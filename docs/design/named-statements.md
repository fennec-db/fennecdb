# Named statements

**Status: built, slices 0 to 7 -- F1 complete.** `require <n> else '<reason>'` (slice 0), the parser and the tree (slice 1), the storage and replication (slice 2), calls and authority (slice 3), HTTP, the router and the upgrade (slice 4), the read-only executor (slice 5), the SDKs (slice 6) and the studio, MCP and the shell (slice 7) are on main: every form below reads into its tree, a definition is a record its database keeps, replicates and holds to its schema, and a call runs through `/query`, `/batch`, `POST /_call/<name>`, every SDK's `call`, an agent's `call_<name>` tool and the studio's call form -- one that writes under the write lock, one that reads beside the other readers, on a replica, a read-only server and a marked read too -- a scoped token's held to its policy's `call` lines and run with the statement's own authority ([what is built](#what-is-built)). The figures past slice 1's are quoted from the design notes they cite, the ones this would owe named with the bench that would take them. The review found the model sound and the first draft too loose in its authority, its storage, its read path and its slices; this revision adopted its findings as the proposal's decisions, and the five that were the owner's are [listed at the end](#decisions-for-the-owner) with the choice taken. F2, write-bound statements, is designed and not built ([below](#write-bound-statements-f2)). Each section's rule is in CLAUDE.md in a few sentences, and this file is its long form, as the others are.

A named statement is a FennecQL statement, or a block of them, defined once in a database and called by name with typed arguments: `create statement transfer($from int, $to int, $amount int) claims (sub text) as begin ... end`, then `POST /_call/transfer` with `{"params": {...}}`, `call transfer {...}` in FennecQL or `db.call('transfer', {...})` in the SDKs. Its authority is its own: a token may be granted `transfer call` with no write rule on the collections it touches. It is fennecdb's answer to PostgreSQL functions used as an API (`SECURITY DEFINER` and PostgREST's `/rpc/`, Supabase's `rpc()`), Convex's mutations and queries and Firebase's callable functions -- without running user code: the body is FennecQL, parsed once, bounded, and run as one block under the single writer.

What it answers. Under a scoped token today the policy is the API: a ledger's app token needs `update(balance)` on `accounts` to move money, and nothing in the policy can say "only as one debit and one credit of the same amount, from an account you own". `require <n>` made a transfer's `/batch` safe against the race ([writes.md](writes.md#require)), but a client holding the grant can still send the debit alone or the credit alone. Transitions ([access.md](access.md#scoped-tokens)) say which way a row may move, never which rows must move together. So an app that wants an operation rather than a grant runs a server of its own in front of fennecdb -- the code fennecdb exists to spare it. A named statement is that operation, kept in the database beside the data it moves.

## Definitions

**A named statement is a record of its database, defined by its text, checked against the schema it reads, and marked broken rather than ever failing an open.** `create statement` writes a record of its own kind (13, `REC_STATEMENT`), a write moving the change counter by one (`writes_in` must count it: its fallthrough, `_ => Ok(0)`, would leave every replica a change behind); it rides in a block, is undone by a variant of its own (`Undo::Statement`), is kept by every image and applied by a replica as a schema record is. Its version is `(history, change)`. A schema change that would break one is refused at its commit, naming it; an internal writer that cannot refuse marks it broken instead.

```fennecql
create statement transfer($from int, $to int, $amount int)
  claims (sub text)
  comment 'Moves an amount from an account the caller owns to another'
  check $amount > 0 and $from != $to
as begin
  get accounts select id where id = $from and owner = $jwt.sub
    limit 1 require 1 else 'not your account';
  set accounts {balance: balance - $amount} where id = $from and balance >= $amount
    require 1 else 'insufficient funds';
  set accounts {balance: balance + $amount} where id = $to
    require 1 else 'no such account';
  insert entries {from: $from, to: $to, amount: $amount, by: $jwt.sub, at: now()}
    returning id, at
end
```

Each statement of that body runs today through the shell as written, literals in place of the parameters, but for `else '<reason>'` (below). A deployment trusting more than one issuer keys `owner` on `sub` and `iss` together -- `sub` is unique only within its issuer, which is why Convex's `tokenIdentifier` joins the two ([Convex](https://docs.convex.dev/auth/functions-auth)) -- and never on a claim a user can edit, an email or a profile's metadata, which an identity provider may let its user change.

**The signature.** A name, then its parameters, its claims and its markers.

- *The name* is ASCII letters, digits and `_`, starting with a letter, at most 59 bytes, in a namespace of its own (a statement and a collection may share one): a path segment with nothing to escape, and `call_<name>` within the 64 characters of the tool names model APIs take (MCP, below).
- *Parameters* are `$name type`, the type one the schema's types name -- `bool`, `int`, `float`, `text`, `bytes`, `timestamp`, `json`, `geo`, `vector<N>`, `sparse<N>`, `[T]` -- read by the parser's `data_type`, so a parameter's type is a field's. One is required and never null unless it has a default, a literal (`$n int = 10`, `$note text = null`). At most 32.
- *Claims* are declared with their types, `claims (sub text, accounts [int])`, the token's top-level claims alone; a name FennecQL does not write as a name is quoted and given one, `claims (groups [text] from "cognito:groups", plan text from "https://example.com/plan")`, the forms Cognito's groups and Auth0's namespaced claims take. A body reads only the claims it declares (`$jwt.sub`, `$jwt.groups`); a nested path into a claim is refused. Declared, a claim's type is checked as an argument's is, and a statement's claims are part of what `describe` shows a caller, since they say which token it takes.
- *`check <expr>`* is a condition over the parameters, the claims and `now()` alone -- a field named in it is refused at definition -- asked before the call takes any lock: a negative amount is a 400 naming the check, never a debit that found the money.
- *`comment '<text>'`* is what `describe`, the MCP tool and generated types say of it.
- *`public`* marks a writing body that reads no claim: one that writes on behalf of nobody in particular -- a vote counted by the poll alone -- must say so, since every token granted it writes the same rows the same way. Without it, such a body is refused at definition.
- *`scans`* acknowledges a writing body whose plan walks a collection no index narrows (below).

**The body** is one statement, or `begin` and statements cut by `;` to `end` -- the shape of PostgreSQL's SQL-standard `BEGIN ATOMIC ... END` body, which [its manual](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/sql-createfunction.html) says "is parsed at function definition time" and "tracks dependencies between the function and objects used in the function body" (it stores the parsed tree, below, which this does not). At most 64 statements, each one of:

- `get` (and SQL's `select`);
- `insert`, and `insert ... if absent`, with its documents written in the text -- an `insert` never writes over a row (409);
- `set ... where` and `del ... where`, or with a pick (`order`, `limit`) -- a row is written over only by a `set` whose filter, in the text, is its guard.

Refused at definition, each with the form to write: `put` in any form, since a `put` naming an id writes over whatever row holds it and an argument could name anyone's; `insert <c> $n` and `put <c> $n`, whose documents -- fields, ids and values -- would be the caller's, past every guard the text holds; `if absent else set` in this first version, whose row is found by a key the caller's document names (a later version may take it where the key is the statement's own); a `set` or a `del` with neither `where` nor a pick (`where true` says it is meant, as REST's `PATCH /<name>/all` does); a schema change (that authority is the server token's, and a statement holding one would hand it to every caller), `compact` and `call` (no recursion to bound, and a body is read whole where it is defined). A body names no collection the database calls its own -- `declared::own`, every name starting with `_`: `_idempotency`, whose rows would let a call forge a replayed answer, `_consumers` and `_follow`, the sync's `_sync_*`, `_migrations`. A name is never a parameter in FennecQL, so a call cannot be pointed at another collection: the trap PostgreSQL's dynamic SQL (`EXECUTE format(...)`) sets has no way in.

**Parameters and claims are names in the text and places in the tree.** The lexer reads `$` followed by a letter as a name (`Tok::Named`) -- `$` and anything but a digit is refused today, so no text that parsed reads otherwise -- and the definition's parser numbers them: the declared parameters first in the signature's order, then the declared claims in theirs, so the body is the same `Expr::Param`s every statement holds and the engine's every path takes it unchanged. `$1` in a body is the first declared parameter, PostgreSQL's "older numeric approach" ([SQL functions](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/xfunc-sql.html)). A named parameter outside a definition is refused ("`$from` names a named statement's parameter; a statement sent alone numbers them, `$1`"); a policy keeps reading `$jwt.<claim>` as it does.

**The answer names its fields.** The body's last statement is what a call answers (below), and it names every field it answers: a `get` has a `select` list, `returning` names its fields, a `lookup` level its own, and `*`, or a `get` with no list, is refused at definition. Run with an authority of its own, a `select *` would start answering a field added to the collection after it was defined -- a `password_hash` -- through every call already granted. PostgreSQL's views expand `*` once, at creation: "columns added later to the table will not be part of the view" ([CREATE VIEW](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/sql-createview.html)); refused rather than expanded here, so the text shown is the answer given. The answer's *types* need an inference pass the engine does not have: `describe`, MCP's `outputSchema` and `fennec types` type a column only where it is a field or a path read as stored (its field's type, a path `json`), `id` or `count` (`int`), and give every other column -- an expression, any other aggregate -- no type until such a pass is built, a slice of its own.

**Checked at definition, and at the commit of every schema change.** At definition the body passes the checks every statement passes before it reads a row -- names against the schema (`Flat::names`, `path_of`), `check_counts`, `check_order`, `check_subquery`, a `lookup`'s child key indexed -- and a plan: a writing body's statement no index narrows (no `and` term an index answers, no pick an ordered walk answers) is refused unless the definition says `scans`, since it would walk the collection under the write lock at every call any granted token makes. Each statement keeps what it depends on: the collections and fields it reads and writes, the indexes its `lookup`s read and the unique indexes its `insert ... if absent` and its `insert`'s duplicate refusal rely on. A schema change -- `alter`, `drop collection`, a field dropped or renamed, an index taken off, `declared::plan`'s apply -- is checked against the statements depending on what it touches *as its block commits*, against the schema the block leaves, not statement by statement: `drop statement transfer; alter collection accounts drop field balance; create statement transfer ...` lands, and so does an `alter` whose block redefines the statement after it. A change leaving one broken is refused naming it ("statement `transfer` reads `accounts.balance`: replace or drop it first"), as PostgreSQL refuses `DROP` over a dependent view without `CASCADE`; a rename is refused too, since a statement's text is never rewritten for it, as `declared::plan` never guesses one. `rebuild`, the declared schema's migration that adds a field again as declared, `set`s it from the old, drops the old and renames the new, is one block, so its commit finds the field under its name again and passes where the statement still checks, and is refused with the plan's resolution where it took away what one relies on. The writers that cannot refuse -- a follower re-copying a collection from its primary, the sync's remake of a collection, a load, a restore -- mark the statements they break broken instead: a broken statement is listed with why, and called it is refused (409) until redefined. Late binding was the alternative -- PL/pgSQL's bodies are strings parsed at their first run -- and a deploy that renamed a field would have broken an API at its next call rather than at the deploy.

**Stored as its text, held to its tree.** The record is `[13][0][length][op][history][change][epoch][digest][name][depends][text]`: `op` a definition or a drop; the text the definition's own bytes from `create` to its end (`spans`), comments and all; `epoch` the grammar's epoch, a number fennec-ql raises whenever a change could read a stored text otherwise; `digest` a hash of the canonical tree the text parsed to; `depends` the names it depends on, so a build that cannot parse it still knows what it touches. An open parses each text again; where the tree's digest is not the one stored -- a later grammar reading the text otherwise, which the corpus rule exists to prevent ([fennecql.md](fennecql.md#sql-spellings): 7 620 texts, none parsed to another tree) -- the statement is marked broken, and calls are refused until it is defined again. An open never fails on a statement: an API's definition must not hold the data hostage. A fixture of stored statements, written by each release and opened by every later binary in its tests -- as `tests/language.fennec` is for the language's features, `FENNEC_WRITE_FIXTURE=1` -- holds each digest across epochs. PostgreSQL stores the tree itself (`prosqlbody`, "pre-parsed SQL function body", [pg_proc](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/catalog-pg-proc.html)), so a column renamed under it follows; storing text, this refuses the rename instead and claims no parity there. A binary from before refuses kind 13, as the file format requires of a new kind ([storage.md](storage.md#file-format)), and so a block holding one -- which sets the upgrade's order (below).

**Replicated, archived, compacted.** The record goes through `wal` and moves the counter by one, counted by `writes_in` as a create's is, so a replica's counter stays the primary's write for write ([replication.md](replication.md#replication)) and applies it as it applies an alter, checking the digest and nothing more; a promotion, a tenant's move, a restore and a read replica carry the statements in the file. An image writes the live definitions after the collections, each with its version, epoch and digest, so a compact keeps them as they were. `/_changes` passes over the record as over a schema record naming no collection's rows -- the cursor moves past it and no row is told -- and a sync replica's stream does too: the server owns its statements, and remaking collections for one would reseed every shape.

**Versions and replacements.** `create statement` refuses a name held (409); `create or replace statement` replaces it and `drop statement [if exists]` drops it, each a schema change: refused to a scoped token and logged by the audit as every `create` and `drop` shape is ([http.md](http.md#audit)). A replacement keeps the statement's contract or is refused: one changing whether it writes, the claims it reads or the fields it answers is a new statement, written as `drop` and `create` -- whose `drop` every grant naming it then visibly names nothing (logged, below) -- since a token granted a statement that read nothing would otherwise start writing, or one keyed on `sub` start reading `groups`, with no line of the policy changed. Whether such a replacement is refused, or allowed and logged as an event of its own, is [the owner's](#decisions-for-the-owner). A version is `(history, change)`: the history the database was on and the change it was defined at -- after a promotion forks the history, a change number alone names two definitions -- carried in the record through compaction, the place an archive's restore names, so a version says both which text ran and where the archive holds it. Every version is in the archive; the file holds the live one. A call runs the definition it finds under the lock it runs under, and a replacement lands under the write lock, so a call runs one version whole. Two versions callable side by side (`transfer@2`) were weighed and left: a change that breaks callers is a new name, `transfer_v2`, as an HTTP API's is.

**Listing and describing.** `statements` lists them, as `collections` lists collections; `describe statement transfer` answers its name, version, parameters with their types and defaults, the claims it reads and their types, its `check`, `comment` and markers, whether it writes and whether it can be held (`Fennec-Wait`, below), the fields it answers with the types it can give, whether it is broken and why, and -- to the server's token alone -- its text and what it depends on. `describe statement` alone stays a collection named `statement`'s description, and `describe statement x` was refused before, so no text that parsed reads otherwise. `GET /_schema` lists them beside the collections, and `?as=fennecql` writes their definitions after the collections.

**Declared in code.** `schema.fennecql` may hold `create statement`s (`fennec_ql::schema_text` reads them after the collections they name), and `declared::plan` compares each with the database's by its canonical tree, never its text -- a comment or a line break is no change -- in the order the migrations leave the schema, so a statement is planned against the collections as the pending migrations will have made them. A statement the database lacks is planned as its `create`; one whose tree differs as its `create or replace`, under the contract rule above (a contract change is reported as the `drop` and `create` it needs, a refusal with its resolution as every other change the plan cannot make alone) -- the one change the plan makes rather than refuses, since a statement holds no data and the code owns its API; one the database holds and the code no longer declares is reported, never dropped, as a collection the code does not declare is left alone. `Mode::Follow` holds them to be there and equal. `integrations/schema-golden.json` gains the cases.

**Limits error.** 64 statements a body, 32 parameters, 16 claims, a name of 59 bytes, 1 000 statements a database, a definition of 64 KB: each refused at definition, never cut ([crates.md](crates.md#limits)). The statements' own limits -- 512 levels of an expression, a `lookup` chain of 8, `in (get ...)` at 100 000 values and 4 levels, `near` and `match` at 10 000 rows -- hold in a body as anywhere.

## Calls

**A call is one block, its arguments values bound by place.** The arguments are checked against the signature where they arrive -- a missing or extra one, a null where none is taken, a value its type refuses: 400, naming it -- and read by each parameter's type as a document's field of that type reads a body's value (`parse_documents_json`: a `json` argument exact, a `vector<N>` its `f32`s, a timestamp from its text or milliseconds); then `check`; then the body runs as `execute_block` runs a `/batch`, every statement with the call's values: its writes land as one record, or none do, and a read in it sees the writes before it ([engine.md](engine.md#blocks)). Nothing is spliced: the body was parsed at definition, and a value is never text again.

**The answer is the last statement's**, as `/query` answers that statement -- the rows of a `get` or a `returning`, a write's count otherwise -- with `Fennec-Seq` and `Fennec-History`; the statements before it answer nothing. PostgreSQL's SQL functions return "the result of the last query in the list" ([SQL functions](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/xfunc-sql.html)); an answer of every statement, as `/batch` gives, would make each guard's rows part of the API.

**Guards, not branches.** FennecQL has no `if` and no loop, and a body gets none: a call holds the write lock for its whole block, so a body whose cost a value could make unbounded would stall every writer behind the single writer ([engine.md](engine.md#single-writer)). What branching an API needs is the block's all-or-nothing over guards: `require <n>` makes a statement's count a condition and puts the block back otherwise ([writes.md](writes.md#require)); a `get ... limit 1 require 1` is "this row exists", a `get` with `require 0` "it does not"; `check` holds the arguments; `insert ... if absent require 1` is "only the first time"; a `where` picks the rows a write applies to and `order ... limit` the one a claim takes. A `like` that counts each user once, over `create index on likes (post, user) @unique`:

```fennecql
create statement like($post int)
  claims (sub text)
as begin
  insert likes {post: $post, user: $jwt.sub} if absent require 1 else 'already liked';
  set posts {likes: likes + 1} where id = $post require 1 else 'no such post'
    returning id, likes
end
```

The statement depends on that unique index -- without it `if absent` would let a user like twice -- so dropping the index is refused while it stands.

**A guard may say why.** `require <n> else '<reason>'` -- a text literal of at most 200 bytes and no backtick, never a parameter, as `n` is never one -- puts the reason in the 412 beside `at`: `{"error": ..., "completed": 0, "at": 1, "reason": "insufficient funds"}`. An API's caller must tell "insufficient funds" from "no such account", and `at` alone made the body's order part of the API, which a statement added in front would shift. It is FennecQL's, not a call's, and lands first, in a slice of its own ([the plan](#the-plan-in-slices)): a `/batch` takes it, `render_batch_stop` and fennec-abi's error carry it beside `at`, every builder's `require` takes a `reason`, held to the golden file, every SDK's error has it, and the sync reports it with a write it puts back. `require 1 else` was refused before it ("unknown command `else`"), so no text that parsed reads otherwise. The shell needs no mark in `Parser::cut` for it: the parser reads `else` straight after the count, so nothing at the text's end is read two ways, and an `else` on the next line goes on with a write as any clause does. Built first, as slice 0 ([writes.md](writes.md#require)).

**A call is a read or a write by its definition, asked under the lock it runs under.** Whether a statement writes is asked before the lock at a dozen places, and each chooses something a wrong answer breaks: which lock, whether a read-only server or a marked read refuses, whether a key is kept, whether a held write may wait, which path the native library and the module take. A call's answer to `is_read_only()` cannot come from its definition there: a replacement keeps whether it writes (its contract, below), but a `drop` and a `create` may land between the question and the lock, and a body judged a read that writes under the read lock is a write with readers beside it. So `Statement::Call` answers `false` everywhere, and a call is asked again under the lock it then runs under, never apart from it (`Database::call_reads`, `Database::reads`). One whose body reads runs through the read-only executor, `Database::read_call`, taking `&Database` -- which cannot write, by its type -- that reads the definition under the read lock it holds and refuses a body that writes (`Error::ReadOnly`), pinned where it is long ([engine.md](engine.md#pinned-reads)): it takes no write lock, keeps no key, opens no block and moves no counter, and a replica, a read-only server and a marked read answer it. One whose body writes takes the write lock, keeps an `Idempotency-Key`, is refused by a read-only server, a replica and a marked read, and is counted a write. Until slice 5 every call was a write. **The definitions in a cell of their own.** The table of statements is an `Arc` of its own in `Database` -- as a collection's schema is (`SchemaCell`) -- so a server reads a definition under a short read lock for what it asks before the lock (its claims, whether it can be held), takes the write lock and runs only where the table is that very `Arc` (`Arc::ptr_eq`), reading it again otherwise, as a REST insert's early body is held to its schema ([engine.md](engine.md#prepared-blocks)); a definition changes the table by a new `Arc`, never in place. Its `put`s are `insert`s with documents in the text, so a call prepares beside the readers as a `/batch` does where they write `PREPARE_AT` documents or more.

**Bounded.** A call examines at most `--call-max-rows` rows (100 000 by default), counted where the scan and the indexes hand rows to its statements: past it the block is put back and the call refused as a query error naming the bound ([crates.md](crates.md#limits)), never answered from the rows it reached. With `scans` at definition, that bound and the write lock are what one call can cost every other writer. A call is held to `--call-rate` a second for its token's subject (`sub` and `iss`), the server's token unheld, answered 429 with `Retry-After` past it: a statement granted to every user is a write path every user can lean on, which the tenant's quota bounds in bytes and rows but not in time.

**Held, keyed and after.** `Fennec-Wait` holds a call as it holds a `/batch` ([waits.md](waits.md#fennec-wait)): a body with no `insert`, whose filters read the time only as `holdable` allows, can be held (`describe`'s `holdable`), its group planned from the body with the arguments and claims bound in (`waits::plan`), each look running `handle` whole -- token, grant and definition again; a replacement or a drop is a schema mark, which wakes every group (`wrote(None)`). A worker given `claim call` and nothing on `jobs` waits for a job with one held request:

```fennecql
create statement claim($queue text)
  claims (sub text)
as set jobs {owner: $jwt.sub, run_at: now() + 30000, attempts: attempts + 1}
   where queue = $queue and run_at <= now() order run_at limit 1
   returning id, payload
```

An `Idempotency-Key` makes a call once as it makes a write once ([http.md](http.md#idempotency-keys)): its block and the key's `_idempotency` row land together, the key the subject's for a scoped token, the request's hash over the path and body, so a retry after a replacement is answered what the first try answered -- the call was made once. `Fennec-After` makes it wait for a change first, as any request.

**Parsed once.** `POST /_call/<name>` parses nothing: the body's tree is the definition's, shared by every call, and run with the call's values where a scoped `/batch`'s statements are copied to have the scope ANDed in ([http.md](http.md#parse-cache)). `call transfer $1` over `/query` is a short text the parse cache keeps.

**Counted and traced by name.** `/_stats/statements` counts a call by the shape `call transfer $1`, whichever surface made it -- its arguments are one parameter -- never its body's statements apart: the call is the unit an operator tunes ([http.md](http.md#statement-counts)). The slow log's `statement` is that shape, its line naming the version; the trace's `db.query.text` is the shape and `db.stored_procedure.name` the name, OpenTelemetry's stable attribute for "the name of a stored procedure within the database" ([registry](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/semconv/registry/attributes/db/)). `/_metrics` counts the route as `call`, never its name: a statement's name is the tenant's API, as a collection's name is the tenant's, and no label names those ([http.md](http.md#metrics)).

## Authority

**A call runs its statement's text with the statement's own authority, and nothing else.** The authority is the text, not a role: there is no owner whose rights a call borrows, so what a statement can do is what it says, fixed when it is defined, and its contract cannot change under a grant (above). A scoped token calls only what a `call` line grants it; its collection rules hold nothing in the body; the caller reaches the body only as the claims the statement declares.

**Granted by name, by role, and to no one by default.** A policy line `<statement>[, <statement> ...] call [for <role>]` grants calling, as every line grants by role:

```text
# statements        access   role
transfer            call     for customer
claim               call     for worker
transfer, refund    call     for teller
```

`call` stands alone on its line -- beside `read` or a `where` it is refused at startup: a grant is all or nothing, as PostgreSQL's `EXECUTE` privilege is. `* call` is refused, with or without a role: a statement defined later would be granted by a line no one reads again, and the policy is the one place that says what a token may do. A line naming a statement the database does not hold is logged as the server starts (on a tenant node, as each tenant opens) -- a typo grants nothing, silently, otherwise -- and so is one whose statement a `drop` took away. A statement a token is not granted is a 404 to it, the answer a statement no database holds gets, so a name tried says nothing -- as a collection no rule grants does not exist for a token ([access.md](access.md#scoped-tokens)). The server's token calls every one. `/_whoami` lists a token's `call` grants. A grant named in a JWT claim (`"calls": ["transfer"]`) was weighed and left: whoever mints tokens picks among the policy's lines by `role` already. So was a `where` on a grant over the arguments (`transfer call where $from in $jwt.accounts`): two places to read what a statement allows, where the statement's own `check` and `where`s are one; two roles with two bounds are two statements.

**Refused in one order.** A call is judged tenant, token, grant, existence, claims, arguments, `check`, each answered before the next is asked. The tenant and the token are `route_tenant`'s pair, as for every route below `/t/<t>/` ([access.md](access.md#tenant-binding)): the token verified (401, with the audit's wait) and held to the tenant it names (403) before the tenant is looked up, so neither says anything of the tenant's statements; the subject's rate is charged once the token is known. Then the grant before the statement is looked up, so an ungranted name and a missing one are the same 404 in the same time; the claims before the arguments, so a token that may never call it is told nothing of the signature's arguments; `check` last, over values already typed.

**The caller is its declared claims.** Each `$jwt.<claim>` a statement declares is bound from the verified token, read exact and held to its declared type; a list claim is spread after `in` and refused past `MAX_CLAIM_VALUES` as a policy's is. A claim the token does not carry, or carries as `null`, as an empty text or an empty list, or as a value of another type, is missing, and the call is refused (403), never run with that value in place: "a filter with a hole in it would match rows it was never meant to", as a policy rule naming a missing claim does not apply (`Access::scope`). Today's policy binding has the same hole for a claim present as `null` or `""` -- `Some(v)` binds it -- which a separate pull request closes there; a statement's binding is written closed from the start. The server's token carries none: it calls a statement reading claims with `as {sub: 'alice'}`, which a scoped token is refused (403) -- the server's token reads and writes everything already, so a call as another user gives it nothing new, and the shell, the studio's admin and a test need it. Convex's functions read `ctx.auth.getUserIdentity()` and Firebase's callable functions get `request.auth` the same way, the authorization written in the function ([Convex](https://docs.convex.dev/auth/functions-auth), [Firebase](https://firebase.google.com/docs/functions/callable)); here it is written in the `where`s and `check`.

**The one statement run outside `within()`.** `scoped()` is where a token meets a statement, and a `call` passes there: the grant asked, the claims bound into the call, `as` refused, and the `append-only` collections held -- a scoped token's call whose body updates or deletes in one is refused (403), since `append-only` takes updates and deletes from every scoped token whatever its rules say ([access.md](access.md#scoped-tokens)), and the server's token keeps corrections there. Then the body runs with no scope installed: the `Check` hook finds none, `fields::hold` and `fields::write` hold nothing, `Hook::answering` passes the answer. CLAUDE.md's "a scoped write run outside `within()` goes unchecked" is this path's point and must stay its only instance. So a scoped token's `/batch` holds no call beside its own statements, and in this first version no more than one call: the hook finds its scope through a thread-local set around the execution, and switching it inside one block is code in the engine, which knows nothing of scopes; and two calls in one block -- each refused by the other's guard, each holding the other's writes -- are a composition whose answers, `at`s and holds this version does not define. The server's token runs any `/batch`.

**The answer is the statement's, not masked by the caller's rules.** A leaderboard over scores no player may read, a balance by an account the caller owns, a job's payload a worker has no `read` on: the statement answers what it names, which is why it must name it. Masking it by the caller's field lists (`Scope::shown`) would make a call's answer depend on who called it -- the property it replaces -- and a statement wanting the caller's rows writes `owner = $jwt.sub` where it reads. Supabase warns that a `security definer` function "can return rows the caller isn't allowed to read" ([database functions](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/database/functions)); here that is the purpose, made safe by the named answer, the grant by name and the text being the whole authority.

**A refusal tells a scoped caller its kind, `at` and `reason` alone.** A body's error message names the collections, fields and values it met -- "document 3 holds `bob@x.io` already" -- which a token that may read none of them is told nothing of, as `access::told` keeps a `@unique` clash to the field. The status stays; the message is the server's, in the trace and, at `--slow-ms`, the slow line, under the request id.

**Bound to its tenant.** The policy and its `call` lines are the node's; the statements are the tenant's, in its file. A call reaches `/t/<t>/_call/<name>` only past `route_tenant`, and a tenant holding no `transfer` answers 404 to a token granted it.

**Audited where schemas are.** `create statement`, `create or replace statement` and `drop statement` are audit events by their shapes, as every `create` and `drop` is; a call is no event, as a write is none, but an MCP tool's (below) is a `mcp` line. Who called is the statement's to write -- `by: $jwt.sub` in the entry the transfer inserts -- which lands in the block, replicates and is restored with the money it moved, where a log line would be beside it.

**Against `SECURITY DEFINER`'s pitfalls.** PostgreSQL's manual and Supabase's guide name them; each has its answer here:

| PostgreSQL's pitfall | Here |
|---|---|
| `search_path`: "malicious users ... creating objects ... that mask objects intended to be used by the function", `pg_temp` searched first ([CREATE FUNCTION](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/sql-createfunction.html)) | One namespace a database, no schemas to search; names checked at definition and at each schema change's commit; only the server's token changes the schema |
| "execute privilege is granted to `PUBLIC` for newly created functions"; Supabase: "any role can call it by default, including `anon`" ([functions](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/database/functions)) | Granted to no scoped token until a line names it; `* call` refused; a 404 until then |
| The owner's privileges, which change after the function is written | No owner: the authority is the text; a replacement keeps the contract and is a logged schema change |
| Dynamic SQL built from arguments | None: parsed once; arguments are values by place; a name is never a parameter; no document an argument makes |
| String bodies parsed at their first run, "the form using string literals may leave dangling functions" | Parsed at definition, held by a digest of its tree; a schema change breaking one is refused at its commit. PostgreSQL's SQL-standard body stores the tree and follows a rename, which this, storing text, refuses -- no parity claimed |
| `SELECT *` gaining columns | The answer names its fields |

**Models weighed.** *Invoker* -- the caller's rules applied to the body, PostgreSQL's and Supabase's default -- gives no statement an authority of its own, the one thing asked; what it adds over sending the text to `/query`, a name and a typed signature, does not pay for a second model whose answers mean different things by caller. *The caller's scope intersected with the definer's* has a customer unable to credit another's account, the one write a transfer is. *A role declared per statement* (`as role ledger`) runs the body under a role's rules: two layers to read for what it may do, where the `where`s and `check` are one. *Definer, as the text, with declared claims* is the least surprising: what the statement does is what is written, every guard is in it, and the token it takes is in its signature.

## Surfaces

**`POST /_call/<name>`**, a body `{"params": {...}}` (none for no arguments), answers as `/query` answers the call's last statement. A path of its own, so a proxy, a firewall's rule or the router in front tells one call from another without reading a body, and an underscore's, as `/_mcp` and `/_schema` are, kept out of every collection's way: PostgREST prefixes `/rpc/` for the same reason, "to avoid route collisions with tables/views of the same name" ([PostgREST](https://docs.postgrest.org/en/stable/references/api/functions.html)). It translates to the `call` statement and runs where FennecQL runs, as every REST route does (`api::route`), and asks `answered_under_lease` as every route answering a write must ([tenants.md](tenants.md#scaling-out-by-tenant)). CORS lets it in as `/query`. GET for a read-only call (PostgREST's "if the function doesn't modify the database, it will also run under the GET method") was left: typed arguments in a query string are texts, and a read goes near by `Fennec-Read: replica` once the read-only executor exists. HTTP stays the one protocol: a call is a request, never an RPC wire of its own. In the order a call is judged:

| Answer | Status |
|---|---|
| no token, or one that does not verify | 401 |
| a token not bound to the tenant | 403, before the tenant is looked up |
| the subject past `--call-rate` | 429, `Retry-After` |
| a statement not granted to a scoped token, or none of that name | 404 |
| a claim the statement declares missing, null, empty or of another type; `as` from a scoped token | 403 |
| an argument missing, extra, null or of another type; `check` false; a scoped `/batch` holding a call beside anything else, or two calls | 400 |
| the body updating or deleting in an `append-only` collection for a scoped caller; a call on a replica, a read-only server or a marked read | 403 |
| a broken statement | 409, with why |
| `insert` over a held id, a `@unique` value taken | 409 |
| `require` unmet: `at`, `reason` | 412 |
| past `--call-max-rows` | 400, naming the bound |
| the `Idempotency-Key` with another request | 422 |
| an MCP answer past `--mcp-max-rows` or `--mcp-max-bytes`, put back | 413 |
| the tenant's quota; above `--max-memory`, a body that inserts or sets, held as the `put` or `set` it holds (`over_ceiling` asks `Database::call_body`), where one that reads or deletes goes on | 507 |
| a lease lapsed | 500, `outcome: unknown` |
| a tenant closed, a ceiling: `retry_after`; a tenant frozen for a move or its lease out, before anything of the call is judged | 503 |
| `Fennec-After` not reached | 504 |
| answered | as `/query`: 200 |

**FennecQL: `call`.** `call transfer {from: 1, to: 2, amount: 300}` takes its arguments as a document, named; `call transfer $1` from a parameter's object, as `put <c> $n` takes documents; `as {sub: 'alice'}` after either, for the server's token. A command word that begins no statement today (refused as "unknown command `call`"), and one more of `TAKEN`, the words no alias takes. `Statement::Call { name, args, claims }` -- `args` a document, a parameter or SQL's positional list, matched to the signature by the engine, since the parser reads no schema. `Database::exactly` walks a call's arguments by its signature, a `json` one read as written.

**SQL: `CALL`, where the meaning holds.** `CALL transfer(1, 2, 300)`, and `name => value` for an argument by name (PostgreSQL's form, [CALL](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/sql-call.html)), are `call`: a stored routine run by name, which may write -- its answer the last statement's rows where PostgreSQL's is the output parameters' row, the one difference, in a column list nobody reads by position. `SELECT transfer(...)` and `SELECT * FROM transfer(...)` are refused naming `call`, without consuming the word: in SQL the first is a function's value in a row and the second a table, and a FROM names a collection here. `CREATE FUNCTION` and `CREATE PROCEDURE` are refused naming `create statement`: PostgreSQL's are `SECURITY INVOKER` unless told, so read as ours they would change who may do what -- the meaning the SQL-spellings rule keeps ([fennecql.md](fennecql.md#sql-spellings)).

**The SDKs.** `db.call('transfer', {from: 1, to: 2, amount: 300}, { idempotencyKey, wait, signal })` in JavaScript, answered as `run` is; Python's `db.call("transfer", {...}, idempotency_key=..., wait=...)`; Go's `db.Call(ctx, "transfer", fennecdb.Params{...})`; .NET's `CallAsync`; Swift's and Kotlin's `call`; Dart's `callStatement` -- a Dart instance method named `call` makes its object callable as a function and lets it be torn off where a `Function` is expected, which a database handle must not be, so Dart takes a name of its own, as it took `openSynced` beside `sync()`. Each sends `call <name> $1` with the arguments as `$1` -- the text a builder renders, held to `integrations/builder-golden.json` with a case for each parameter type, for `reason` and for `as` -- over `/query`, so the transport, the errors, the key and `wait` are what every SDK has. With the read-only executor built (slice 5), a call text an SDK already sends marked -- `run('call history $1', [args], { read: true })` and its kin -- goes near and is answered there; `call`'s own `read` option is that mark, built with the method (slice 6). `call` was chosen over `run`, which each SDK holds already for a text, and over `rpc`: FennecQL's word, SQL's `CALL`, the route, the grant and the method are one word. `fennec types` writes each statement's arguments, and its answer as far as it is typed, in every language it writes.

**MCP: a tool a statement.** With `--mcp`, each statement the token may call is a tool named `call_<name>`. MCP's own rule takes dots ([tools](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-11-25/server/tools)), but the clients hand a tool's name on to a model's API, and Claude's takes `^[a-zA-Z0-9_-]{1,128}$` ([define tools](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/define-tools)) and OpenAI's function names `^[a-zA-Z0-9_-]{1,64}$`: no dot, and 64 characters at most, which `call_` and a 59-byte name fit. The tool's `description` is the statement's `comment`; its `inputSchema` the signature (`required` the parameters with no default, `additionalProperties: false`); its `outputSchema` the answer as `{"rows": [...]}`, its columns typed as `describe` types them; `readOnlyHint` and `destructiveHint` from what the body does. A writing statement is a tool only under `--mcp-write`, as every write tool is. A curated statement is the safest tool an operator can hand an agent: a model's arguments go in as values, never as text, the rule every tool keeps ([http.md](http.md#mcp)). The answer is held to `--mcp-max-rows` and `--mcp-max-bytes` before its block lands: past them a write is put back and the call refused (413), so a model is never told half an answer nor leaves a write it was not told of. `--mcp-calls-per-sec` holds them, and a writing one is a `mcp` audit line. fennecdb's MCP answers one JSON object and never streams, and declares `listChanged: false` as it does now: a client lists again.

**The studio** lists the statements in the schema view -- the server's token sees each definition, why one is broken, and its `/_schema/plan` as replaced; a scoped token the signatures it may call -- and a call form made from the signature, its inputs typed, answers in the grid; a call is sent under an `Idempotency-Key`, as the studio's writes are, written in `studio/statements*.js` under their rules: arguments as one parameter, the name as FennecQL writes one ([http.md](http.md#studio)). Two pieces of the studio read FennecQL's text themselves and must learn `begin ... end`: the editor's `splitStatements` (`statements-views.js` 26), which cuts at every `;` outside strings and comments and would send a definition's body as separate statements, and the highlighter, `site/highlight.js`, copied in by `make studio-highlight`, whose words gain `begin`, `end`, `call`, `statement`, `claims` and the markers.

**The shell** reads `call` as a write (`kind`: it waits for an empty line when typed over lines), `create`/`drop statement` as schema changes, `statements` and `describe statement` as reads. A body over lines needs no bracket of `lexer::Unclosed`: the parser refuses an unended `begin` at the text's end, which the shell reads as "go on". `call transfer` at a line's end is a mark in `Parser::cut` (its document may come on the next line) -- `require <n>`'s `else` needs none, read straight after the count; each form takes a line of `a_statement_written_over_lines_reads_as_one`'s corpus ([fennecql.md](fennecql.md#the-shell)).

## Replicas, sync, the router and upgrades

**A replica answers a call that reads, and refuses one that writes.** A follower answers a body of `get`s from the statements in its file, as the read-only executor reads it (above); a read-only server does too, and a marked read (`Fennec-Read: replica`) of one goes near through the router like any marked read and is answered there ([read-replicas.md](read-replicas.md#marked-reads)), at the change and history that copy read at, as every marked read is. One that writes is still 403 (`Error::ReadOnly`) before any of it runs -- judged first on a read-only server and a marked read, as slice 4 ordered it -- and a marked one is refused at the replica, never sent home to land. Until slice 5 every call was refused there.

**A sync replica refuses `call` and `create statement`, in both runners, from the slice that brings the syntax.** A sync replica holds its shapes, not its server's file, and none of its statements: its rows are a filtered part of the server's, so the transfer's credit would find no account the shape does not hold and its `require` would count the replica's rows -- the reason it already refuses a `get`'s `require` beside a synced write (`GUARDED`), a write reaching an unanswered insert's row (`REQUIRE_UNANSWERED`), a picked write (`PICKED`) and an upsert naming no key (`UPSERT_KEY`) ([writes.md](writes.md)). So in this first version a synced database refuses both by a code of their own, the native core and `FennecSync` alike, each a scenario of `integrations/sync-scenarios.json` before either runner changes ([native-and-sync.md](native-and-sync.md#sync-scenarios)); an app calls its server through its HTTP client, and what the call wrote reaches the replica through the shapes' streams as any server write does. Forwarding a call from the replica is [a follow-up](#forwarding-calls-from-a-sync-replica).

**The router forwards a call, and parses a tenant's schema.** `/t/<tenant>/_call/<name>` is a tenant path, forwarded as bytes; as a `POST` it is sent again only where `replayable` allows -- keyed ([router.md](router.md#pooled-connections)). The global entry passes it as any `/t/<tenant>/` path. But "the router parses no FennecQL" holds for requests, not for a tenant's creation: `PUT /_shard/tenants/<t> {schema}` reads the schema text before anything is placed (fennec-shard's `create`, through `fennec_http::admin::schema_description`, `lib.rs` 4716), so a schema holding `create statement` needs a router that knows the grammar, and a router from before refuses it as a schema that does not read (400) -- loudly, with nothing placed. A query across tenants is read-only on every node: a call whose statement reads on each tenant is answered and merged as any read, and one whose statement writes is refused before it runs, naming it -- "a query across tenants reads, and `call stamp` writes" (`a_call_across_tenants_reads_and_one_that_writes_is_refused`).

**Upgrades go one way, every reader before the first definition.** A binary from before refuses record kind 13 and a block holding one, so the first `create statement` on a primary stops every reader of its file that is older: a replica following its stream, an archiver writing it, a node a tenant is moved or restored to, a standby a failover would promote. The order, written into RELEASING.md's notes for the release and `deploy/RUNBOOK.md`'s *Upgrading with no downtime*, is that runbook's own: archivers, then the routers, then every node -- each replica and standby, each node a move, a restore or a failover may put a tenant on -- and only then may any primary define a statement. What it holds is the last step: every binary that reads a tenant's file, the archivers and the nodes, runs the new binary before the first definition. A router reads no tenant's file, only a schema's text as it creates a tenant, and one from before refuses a schema declaring a statement (400, nothing placed), so its place among them can lose nothing; it keeps the place every upgrade gives it, ahead of the nodes, rather than an order of its own for this release. The runbook says already that files go one way and an older node may not follow a newer stream; this is the first feature whose first use, not its install, moves the format, so the release notes name the statement that does. A test drives the order (`make upgrade-check`, `tools/upgrade-order.sh`, against the binaries of the last main before the kind): each one from before meets the record and stops with a refusal in words, never a misread -- measured, the replica refuses the write and connects again, never past it; the archiver keeps archiving the stream's bytes but takes no image of its own, and the old `verify` and `restore` refuse the archive, which the new ones read; the old server refuses to open the file the new `restore` wrote; the old router refuses the schema (400) and places nothing; and a move onto an old node fails, the tenant left on its source with what it held ([what is built](#what-is-built), slice 4).

## The browser module and embedded

**The record is core in every build; defining and calling are measured, and refused in the shipped module unless they fit the budget.** A file a server wrote opens in a page (`openFile`) and in an app, and a build must open every file and keep what it holds through its compacts ([browser-module.md](browser-module.md#language-features)), so every build keeps the record -- reads it, writes it again in its images, applies it from a replica, lists its name, version and whether it is broken -- with no parser of its own needed: a build that cannot parse a statement keeps its text as it came, and a schema change touching what its `depends` names marks it broken, as an internal writer does (the holder of the file holds everything; the server that opens the file again refuses its calls until redefined). Defining and calling -- the signature's parse and typing, the checks, the dependency checks at commit, the plan's comparison, the call's typing and its refusal texts, `describe`'s JSON -- the review estimates at 6-9 KB brotli of the module, above the first draft's 3.5-5, which left out the refusal texts, the dependency checks, the plan and `describe`; for scale, upserts grew the module 1.3 KB brotli, picked writes 1.9, an `alter` 4.0 ([writes.md](writes.md), [fennecql.md](fennecql.md#alter-collection)). Measured in their slice (`make wasm-sizes`, `make size-report`), they ship in the module only within the budget [the owner sets](#decisions-for-the-owner); otherwise the module refuses `call` and `create statement` with words of their own ("named statements are a server's; this module is built without them"), and an embedder needing them uses the native library or a server.

**How the code leaves the module, not merely a feature inside `full`.** The module ships "with every feature" ([browser-module.md](browser-module.md#index-features)), and a crate using fennec-core names `full`, so a feature `full` turned on would still ship. Defining and calling are a fennec-core and fennec-ql feature, `statements`, that `full` does not include: `fennec-http`, `fennec-ffi`, `fennec-cli` and `fennec-server` name it, `fennec-wasm` and the module's `make wasm` do not, `fennec-abi` names it only in its dev-dependencies (its calls pass `Statement::Call` to the engine and hold no code of their own), and `off.rs` refuses a call by name in a build without it (`not_built`). `make wasm-sizes` reports it in its table beside the index features. A Durable Object running the module then has no calls -- the one use the first draft made the module's case on -- until the budget lets them in.

**The embedded API** is a statement, not an export: `call transfer $1` through `Database::execute_with` and `fennec_query`, so no C ABI, no JNI entry and no `fennec.h` line is new. `Database::call(name, args, claims)` is the engine's path the statement runs (arguments by name or place, claims the values of the declared claims). The native SDKs' local `call` (Swift's and Kotlin's `call`, Dart's `callStatement`) take `claims` -- the embedder's `as` -- since an app may call a statement reading `$jwt.sub` with its signed-in user, held to the declared types as a token's are. `Fennec-Wait` is a server's: the local calls take no `wait`, and the page's `Fennec.call`, which takes one as `run` does, refuses it (`LOCAL_WAIT`), as on any local write.

## What it does not do

No user code: no JavaScript, WASM or Lua in the engine. Every crate but `fennec-bench` has zero dependencies ([crates.md](crates.md#zero-dependencies)), and an interpreter of its own would be the largest code and attack surface the server holds, under a write lock a single writer shares with every client. No loops or branches (above); no network calls -- a call holds the write lock, and a wait on a socket under it stalls every writer, which is why Convex's mutations "must be deterministic, and cannot call third party APIs" ([mutations](https://docs.convex.dev/functions/mutation-functions)); no variables carrying one statement's result into the next; no schedule. An API needing a statement's result in the next takes a key from its caller -- a `@unique` text the client makes, which the statements after the `insert` find the row by -- until a follow-up binds results to names. Four follow-ups would reuse the definition machinery -- the record, the checks, the authority as the text, the surfaces -- each a proposal of its own.

**F2, write-bound statements.** A statement bound to the writes of a collection, `after set orders where new.status = 'paid'`, run in the block of the write that matched it with that row as `new.` and `old.`: derived rows -- a counter, a ledger line, a denormalized field -- kept with the write that made them, PostgreSQL's trigger without its language and PocketBase's `onRecordAfterUpdateSuccess` without its JavaScript. It inherits the danger PostgreSQL's CVE-2020-25695 and CVE-2022-1552 are: code with one authority run by another's action -- there, functions and triggers a user created, run with a superuser's identity by `ANALYZE`, `REINDEX`, `CREATE INDEX`, `VACUUM FULL`, autovacuum or a restore, "an attacker having permission to create non-temp objects in at least one schema could execute arbitrary SQL functions under a superuser identity" ([CVE-2020-25695](https://www.postgresql.org/support/security/CVE-2020-25695/), [CVE-2022-1552](https://www.postgresql.org/support/security/CVE-2022-1552/)). Here a bound statement would run with its own authority when a scoped token writes, and when the server's own writers -- the sweeper, a migration, `--follow`'s mirror, a restore -- write. It owes: definition by the server's token alone, as every statement; never run by an internal writer (a replica's apply, a load, a restore, a compact, the sweeper replays nothing, since the record holds what the statement wrote); a depth of one (its writes bind nothing further); a bound on the rows one write may wake; the plugin `Hook` as the place it runs; and a sync replica that does not run it optimistically but receives what it wrote through its streams. Designed [below](#write-bound-statements-f2), which keeps each of these but the place it runs: the engine runs it at the end of the statement that woke it (`run_one`), since a hook has no database to write with and runs inside its statement's loop over rows, and the `Hook` is where a policy meets it (`Hook::waking`, `Hook::writer_only`).

**F3, scheduled statements.** `create statement purge() public every 1h as del sessions where seen < now() - 86400000`, run by the primary alone as `@ttl`'s sweeper is -- never a replica, a following tenant or the browser ([indexes.md](indexes.md#ttl)) -- each run a block keyed by its slot, so a failover between a run and its answer runs it once; one behind its schedule runs once, not once a slot missed; held to the tenant's quota and `--call-max-rows`, its time out of the slow log as a held write's is. It owes the clock it reads (`now()` folded once a run), a last-run record that replicates, and the router knowing nothing of it.

**F4, webhook consumers.** An inbound endpoint, `POST /_hooks/<name>`, verified by a signature its sender makes -- an HMAC of the body under a secret, as Stripe and GitHub sign theirs -- and bound to a statement's parameters from the body, keyed by the event's id so a sender's retries land once. The secret is the policy's or a key file's, never the database's, so an archive holds no credential; the statement is a named statement with an authority of its own, its caller the sender, its claims none (`public`).

### Forwarding calls from a sync replica

A later version may let a synced database's `call` go to its server rather than refuse it. Three things the first draft passed over make it a proposal of its own. *When it settles*: the call's promise would settle once the replica holds what it wrote, but a call's writes are known by the collections its body writes, not by a seq a shape is sure to pass -- a body writing a collection no shape follows moves no stream -- so it settles by the shapes over the collections the definition says it writes, each past the answer's `Fennec-Seq`, and at once where none is followed. *Its place in the queue*: sent after the writes made before it, a call is a barrier in `_sync_queue` -- the writes after it wait for its answer, since one may read what it wrote -- which the queue, a line of independent writes each with its undo, does not have. *The native path*: `fennec_query` returns a native write once kept, where a call has nothing to keep and must wait for its server, so the native core needs an asynchronous action and answer it does not have (`fennec_sync_feed`'s actions are the binding's to perform, its writes answered at once). Each is a scenario first, both runners.

**Also left:** live calls -- a read-only call as a live query (`FennecHttp.live` polls a text by its content tag already, and `call board $1` is one) -- needs the read-only executor and the collections a call reads named to the client; `if absent else set` and `put` in a body, where the key or the id is the statement's own; `insert <c> $n` with the documents' fields fixed by the statement; a scoped `/batch` of several calls; a parameter's type named after a field (`$amount accounts.balance`, PostgreSQL's `%TYPE`); the answer's type inference.

## What is built

**Slice 1, the parser and the tree.** Every form of this proposal reads into its tree: `create [or replace] statement` with its signature, claims, markers, `comment` and `check`, a body of one statement or `begin ... end`; `drop statement [if exists]`, `statements`, `describe statement <name>`; `call` with a document, a parameter or SQL's list, and `as`. The tree is `fennec_core::named` -- `Definition`, `Param`, `Claim`, `Call`, `Args` -- and the body's refusals that need nothing but its text (`Definition::refused`: a `put`, a parameter's documents, `else set`, a `set` or `del` with neither `where` nor a pick, a schema change, `compact`, `call`, a `_` collection, an answer naming no fields, a writing body reading no claim and not `public`) are answered as the definition is read, each at the statement it is about. `$name` and `$jwt.<claim>` are `Tok::Named`, numbered by the definition's parser (`Parser::named`) and refused outside one; the rest of the language reads as on main.

*Nothing runs one.* `Statement::is_named` is asked before a block opens (`execute_block`, `execute_with`), so a `/batch` holding a `call` behind a `put` lands nothing; `query` refuses them too; `scoped()` refuses a `call` before it asks a rule of the token (a definition and a drop are schema changes, refused to a scoped token as ever); MCP's `write` refuses a `call`. Each answers `NOT_BUILT`, "named statements are not built yet", a query error (400), its words a client tells it by. `statements` answers no row and `describe statement` 404, for every token. A sync replica meets the same refusal -- the native core passes the statement to the engine, FennecSync runs on the module, which reads none -- until slice 3 gives each runner its own code and scenario.

*The module.* Named statements are a feature, `statements`, of fennec-core (the variants of `Statement` and `fennec_core::named`, and the engine's answers) and of fennec-ql (their parse, turning on fennec-core's), outside `full`. fennec-ql takes it by default, and the workspace's dependency without: fennec-http, fennec-cli and fennec-ffi name it (fennec-server and fennec-shard through fennec-http), fennec-abi in its dev-dependencies and its `sync` feature, which the native library alone turns on (slice 3, to refuse them on a sync replica), and fennec-wasm does not. The module refuses `create [or replace] statement`, `drop statement`, `statements`, `call` and a `$` before a letter (`$a`) in words of their own, "named statements are a server's; this module is built without them", before anything of the text runs, and reads `describe statement x` and SQL's routines as main did (`web/fennec.test.js`). The tree's variants were core in the first cut and cost the module 1 807 bytes brotli (6 385 raw) refusing all of it -- `Statement`'s drop glue, the engine's arms, the parser's refusals of what only a server reads -- and gated 481 raw bytes, gzip and brotli within their noise (`make size-report BASE=origin/main`): the dispatch of the five refusals, the lexer's of `$from`, the parser's one field. Slice 2's record is core in every build, with the code that keeps it; defining and calling stay in the feature. Turned on in the module, slice 1's parse alone weighs 28.6 KB raw and 8 668 bytes brotli -- the parse of the signature and body, `Definition::refused`, the canonical encoder and their words -- against the 3 KB brotli budget suggested for all of defining and calling ([the owner's decision 5](#decisions-for-the-owner)): it stays out. The server pays for them: `fennec-server`'s code is 39.2 KB more, 3 076.6 KB to 3 115.8 (fennec-ql 16.9, fennec-core 13.5, `core`'s formatting 8.1), its build 62 KB of 5 298 (`make size-report BIN=fennec-server`, on main and the branch).

*Parse time.* The parse of the repository's texts that are no definition -- 8 909 of the corpus below, `parse` alone, 40 rounds, the median of nine runs -- took 1 305-1 310 ns a text on the branch against 1 303-1 306 on main, in turns, the feature off 1 306-1 312: as main's. The first cut measured 1.6% slower: the definition's paths inline in the lexer's loop, `primary`, `put`'s documents and a `get`'s collection weighed on texts holding none, and each went out of line, `#[cold]`.

*Size and stack.* Each variant past a select's size is boxed (`CreateStatement(Box<Definition>)`, `Call(Box<Call>)`): a `Statement` is 968 bytes on main and on the branch, a `Select`'s 960 and its tag, held by `a_statement_is_as_large_as_a_select`. 511 parentheses in a body or a `check` parse within the stack 511 take at the top -- 1 011 KiB in release, 7 282 KiB in a debug build against 7 266 -- a definition's frames above its body's statement a few; 512 are refused as at the top.

*The digest and the epoch.* `Definition::canonical` writes the tree as tagged fields, each written only where it holds something -- so a field added to the tree later leaves every stored digest as it was (`a_field_that_holds_nothing_is_not_written`) -- and `digest` hashes it; `replace` and `block`, how a definition was written rather than what it means, are left out, and so is every literal's spelling but its bits (`0.0` and `-0.0` apart). A guard's reason is a field of its own beside its count, and an operator, `CmpOp`'s and `ArithOp`'s, is a byte of its own (`cmp_byte`, `arith_byte`), never its variant's place, so an enum reordered or grown moves no digest. `fennec_ql::GRAMMAR_EPOCH` is 1; the transfer's digest is pinned in `the_digest_holds_the_tree_not_the_text`, so a change of the encoding fails there and raises the epoch with it.

*The corpus.* `make fennecql-corpus BASE=origin/main` (`tools/fennecql_corpus.py`, `crates/fennec-ql/examples/corpus.rs`) harvests every FennecQL text the repository tracks -- the docs' blocks and inline code, the golden files and the sync's scenarios, the string literals of every SDK's sources and the crates' -- and parses each with `parse` and `parse_exact` in this tree and in BASE's, comparing the trees' `Debug` forms. Against main: 9 122 texts, 18 244 parses, no tree changed and none that parsed refused; 68 parse that were refused (this branch's own texts and docs, the design's examples, and two that were never FennecQL, a .NET example's "Call mom" and the studio's label `Statements`), and 190 are refused in other words. The texts reworded are those `$` and a letter, `select f(...)` with no `from` and `call` reached: `$LIMIT` in a template is now refused as a named parameter rather than as no number, and `select count(*) where ...`, its collection left out, is told both readings, `select count(...) from <collection>` and `call count(...)`.

*The words.* `call` and `statements` begin statements and reserve no word: SQL reserves neither (PostgreSQL's `CALL` is a word any alias takes), so they are not in `COMMANDS`, the words no alias may be, but in `NAMED_COMMANDS`, the words no named statement's name may be. `select a from t call`, `select a call from t`, `select count(*) statements from t`, `select c.a, call.b from t c join calls call on call.id = c.id` and `select a from t statements` read as on main, in the module too. At the text's end a `call` with no name after it is not consumed, so `select a call` with `from t` to come is one statement, not a `get` and a `call`; a SQL statement followed by a `call` takes a `;` between them, as in PostgreSQL.

**Slice 2, storage and replication.** A definition is record kind 13 (`engine/stored.rs`, `REC_STATEMENT`), `[13][0][length][op][history][change][epoch][digest][name][depends][text]`, op 1 a definition and 2 a drop (no epoch, digest, depends or text); an op, or a byte past the text, this version does not read is refused as corrupt, never read as something else. It is one write: `writes_in` counts it, alone or among a block's records (`each_inner`), a replica's `apply` notes it as a schema change of no collection (`SCHEMA_MARK` on collection 0), and its version is `(History::current, the change it takes)`, the open block's writes before it counted. The table is `Database::statements`, an `Arc<Statements>` by name, changed by a new `Arc` wherever a reader holds the old one, and put back in a block by `Undo::Statements`, the table before. An image writes each live definition after the collections, version, epoch and digest as they were; a compact beside the database writes the table it took and refuses to swap where the table is another by then (`Arc::ptr_eq`), as for a collection changed meanwhile; `adopt` takes the image's. `changes_in` hands the record over as `ChangeKind::Statement` of no collection, which `/_changes` passes over, its cursor with it.

*The open's digest check.* fennec-core cannot parse, so `fennec_ql::install` gives the process its reader (`named::read_with(fennec_ql::definition)`): fennec-server's and the shell's `main`, `fennec_http::Server::new` and the native library's open call it. An open, an image adopted and a replica's schema change read each statement not read yet: its text parsed, the tree's digest held to the stored one and the tree kept for a call; a text that no longer reads, or reads to another digest, marks it broken ("its text reads as another statement under grammar epoch 1, where it was defined under 1: define it again") until it is defined again, and fails nothing. A process that installed no reader keeps each unread, written again by every image. The record's text is `Definition::text`, the definition's own bytes from `create` to its end, which the parser fills for a definition alone (`definition_text`, `#[cold]`); the digest, `PartialEq` and `Debug` leave it out, so `make fennecql-corpus` compares the trees it compared. `GRAMMAR_EPOCH` moved into `fennec_core::named`, beside the encoder whose change raises it, and `fennec_ql::GRAMMAR_EPOCH` is it.

*Dependencies.* `Definition::depends` reads them off the tree against the schema: each collection named -- refused where none holds it -- each of their fields it reads or writes that the collection holds (a name it does not hold, an alias or a join's, is the statement's own checks' in slice 3), the index each `lookup` probes (`c.f@hash`, `c(a,b)@` an index over several fields leading with the keys) -- refused where none answers, as running it would be -- and the unique indexes of each collection it inserts into (`c.f@unique`, `c(a,b)@unique`), whose refusal of a duplicate an `if absent` and a `require` are written around. Stored as names, so a build that cannot parse the text knows what it touches. A block holding a schema change, on a database holding statements, holds each statement not broken to the schema the block leaves as it lands (`statements_hold`, before the fence): one whose dependency is gone is refused naming it -- "statement `transfer` reads `accounts.balance`: replace or drop it first", a query error (400), PostgreSQL's dependent view without `CASCADE` -- or, from a writer that cannot refuse, marked broken: `Database::internally`, which `--follow`'s copy again and the sync's remake run their drops under, and a replica's apply. A rebuild's block finds the field under its name again and lands; a block replacing or dropping the statement before the change lands. The plan's check behind `scans`, the names a statement does not hold and the types of what it reads are the definition's checks in slice 3, which runs a body's statements' own checks.

*A mark is derived, never stored.* It lasts while the schema lacks what the statement names and is cleared once the schema holds it again -- the collection made again under its name -- where the proposal said "until redefined": a mark kept in memory and lost at the next open would have made the same file broken or not by the process's history, so every open, apply and schema change finds it again from the schema -- a `create index` built beside the database (`Database::maintain`) too, which lands its schema change outside `commit` (`finish_index`, `finish_composite`): left to the next open, its mark outlived the index it lacked on the primary while a replica applying the same record cleared it (`an_index_built_beside_the_database_clears_the_mark_on_the_primary_as_on_a_replica`); a misread text's mark depends on the text and the grammar alone, and lasts. A build without the feature -- the browser module -- reads the record, keeps it, writes it again in its images and applies it from a replica, its text as it came, and reads no tree, checks no dependency and marks nothing: no such build lists or calls by a mark, and the server that opens a file the module changed finds what each statement lacks. The design asked the module to mark by `depends`; done there, the check was 1.7 KB of the module for a mark nothing reads.

*Definitions run where a test lets them.* `Database::allow_definitions` (`#[doc(hidden)]`) lets `create statement` and `drop statement` run on that database; everywhere else they stay `NOT_BUILT`, and every `call`. So nothing a server or an app runs writes kind 13 -- a statement nobody can call, and the first record an older reader of the file would stop at -- and the upgrade's order (slice 4) is not owed yet. Defined, a name held is 409 without `or replace`; a database holds 1 000 (`MAX_STATEMENTS`) and a text 64 KB (`MAX_TEXT`), refused past either; and a replacement keeps the contract or is refused naming the `drop` and `create` it needs (`Definition::breaks_contract`: whether it writes, its claims and their types, the fields it answers -- [the owner's decision 3](#decisions-for-the-owner), its recommended choice taken). It is held to the old tree wherever there is one, a statement marked broken by what the schema lacks included, as `declared::plan_statements` holds it: the mark is the schema's, and a grant names the statement whatever the schema holds, so a broken `transfer` replaced by a read of other claims is refused as a whole one is (`a_broken_statement_is_replaced_only_within_its_contract`). A text that misreads keeps no tree, and is defined again as anything.

*Declared.* `fennec_ql::schema_into` reads a schema text's `create statement`s after the collections each names (refused before them, or declared twice); `schema_text` reads the same and answers the collections. `declared::plan_statements`, beside `plan` rather than around it so the module's apply keeps its one copy of the plan, compares each with the database's by digest: one missing is its text, one changed its `create or replace` where the contract holds and `statement_contract_changed` where not, and under `Mode::Follow` `statement_missing` and `statement_changed`, each refusal's `collection` the statement's name. One the database holds and the code does not declare is left as it is, as a collection is, rather than "reported": the plan has no field for a note, and a refusal would hold up the apply. Applying a planned definition meets `NOT_BUILT` until slice 3, and a tenant's schema declaring one is refused at the router before anything is placed (`admin::schema_description`), as a router from before refused it as a schema that does not read. `integrations/schema-golden.json` gains no case, since the module writes it and reads no definition; fennec-http's `/_schema/plan` plans the collections alone, since what a scoped token is shown of a statement is slice 3's.

*Tests.* `crates/fennec-core/tests/stored.rs`: the record and its count alone and in a block, a block put back, every open and image, a compact read in and mapped, a replica and a reader of the changes, the refusals naming the statement with the replacement first in one block landing, a rebuild, the marks of `internally`, a replica and an open, a definition's own refusals, and the fixture `tests/statements.fennec` (`FENNEC_WRITE_FIXTURE=1`), whose `stale` record holds a digest no tree of its text has; fennec-ql's `a_definition_keeps_its_own_text` and `a_schema_text_declares_statements_after_their_collections`; fennec-abi's `a_schemas_statements_are_planned_by_their_trees`; and the module opening the fixture and keeping every text through its images after a schema change took what one reads (`web/fennec.test.js`). The upgrade's test -- an archiver and a replica built without kind 13 stopping in words -- is slice 4's, beside the order it holds: a replica from before stops at "record kind 13 is not a write" (`apply_records`), an open at "unknown record kind 13" (`load_records`) or "a block holds a record that is no write" (`each_inner`), and an archiver from before counts the record as no write (`writes_in`'s fallthrough), which is why readers upgrade first.

*Size.* The browser module: +4 116 bytes raw, +1 706 gzip and +824 brotli (`make size-report BASE=origin/main`; `fennec-lite` +1 286 brotli, no feature +1 379) -- the record read (`take_statement`, 1.6 KB raw), the table's clone and its image. The first cut was +6 576 brotli: the dependency check and its words in the module, `str` slices that could panic and brought a `char`'s formatting in, and the plan's function taking the mode at run time, which brought `Mode::Follow`'s copy of the plan into the module. `fennec-server`'s code is 57.4 KB more, 3 128.2 KB to 3 185.6, its build 78 KB of 5 404 (`make size-report BIN=fennec-server`, on main and the branch): slice 1's canonical encoder, which nothing reached until a digest was taken, 17.9 KB; the record, its table, the open's read and the commit's check 14.8; the dependency walk 9.4; the replacement's contract 6.4; the plan 2.2. A `Definition` cloned into the table was 5.7 KB more; the tree is read back from the text instead, as an open reads it.

*Time.* A commit asks an empty table one question (`statements.is_empty()`) and a replica's apply none -- a block of 50 000 writes would have had its notes walked for a schema mark; the parse compares each statement's kind once, for a definition's text. The parse's corpus time against main, slice 1's measurement, was not taken: the machine stayed loaded past 11 for the twenty minutes it was given, and a run under someone else's load is no figure; it is owed to a quiet one.

*The shell.* `call` is a write in fennec-cli's `kind`, `drop statement` and a `begin ... end` definition schema changes, `statements` and `describe statement` reads, a definition of one statement a write, since its `where` may come on the next line. An unended `begin` is refused at the text's end, which the shell reads as "go on", with no bracket of `lexer::Unclosed`; `call <name>` at a line's end is a mark in `Parser::cut`, its document on the next line, and so is `describe statement`, the collection `statement`'s description alone and a named statement's with a name after it: the reading says so (`Reading::open`), and the shell holds it for the next line where it would run a read at Enter. Not every mark holds a statement: most are left by a reading tried and dropped -- `get notes count` tries SQL's list first -- and a read the next line may go on with runs at Enter all the same; each form is a line of `a_statement_written_over_lines_reads_as_one`'s corpus, and `tests/shell.rs` types and pipes a definition over lines.

**Slice 3, calls and authority.** Definitions and calls run wherever FennecQL runs -- `/query`, `/batch`, the shell, the native library and a declared schema's apply; `Database::allow_definitions` is gone. What slices 4 to 7 build keeps refusing: MCP's `write` refuses a `call` and a router a tenant's schema declaring one (`NOT_BUILT`, now "named statements are not built here yet: ... run through `/query` and `/batch`"), `Fennec-Wait` holds no call (`waits::holdable`), and `/_call/<name>` is no route -- each but MCP's built by slice 4, below. A sync replica refuses `call`, `create statement` and `drop statement` in both runners in words of their own (`NAMED`, a scenario of `integrations/sync-scenarios.json` first; [native-and-sync.md](native-and-sync.md#sync-core)): the native core reads them (fennec-abi's `sync` feature turns on fennec-ql's `statements`), and FennecSync tells the module's refusal of them in those words on its replica.

*The call* (`engine/call.rs`). `Database::callee` judges one, each answered before the next is asked: the statement there (404, `NotFound`), the claims it declares (403, `Denied`), the arguments (400), `check` (400), a mark (409, `Exists`, with why) -- of what the schema lacks, or of a text that reads here as another statement, which is judged by the tree it reads as (`Stored::misread`) and never run. The first cut answered a text that no longer read before its claims, so a caller lacking them was told the statement was broken; only a text this build reads as nothing at all, or a process that installed no reader, has no claim to judge, and is refused at once. `Database::call` then runs each statement of the body with `run_one` in the block its caller opened -- a lone call's own, a `/batch`'s -- with the values by place, the arguments in the signature's order and then the claims, as the parser numbered them; the answer is the last statement's. `Statement::Call` is a write wherever it is asked, and the read path refuses it ("requires write access"). The arguments come by name (`{...}`, `$n`'s object, SQL's `name =>`) or by place, each read by `Value::coerce` as a document's field of its type is; one missing, extra, given twice, of another type, or `null` where the parameter has no default (the design's "never null unless it has a default", read as written) is refused naming it. `Database::exactly` walks a call's arguments and claims by the signature (`call_exactly`), so a `json` or `geo` one, in the text or a parameter, is read as written.

*The claims.* A call carries the server's token's `as {...}`, or a scoped token's own claims, which `Scope::rewrite` binds into it whole; each claim the statement declares is read by its name (`from "cognito:groups"`), and one missing, `null`, `""`, an empty list, an object, a list holding any of those, or of a type its declaration refuses, is one the caller lacks: 403 naming the claim, nothing run (the policy's `usable`, an empty list refused too). A list of more than `named::MAX_CLAIM_VALUES` (1 000, the one number `access::MAX_CLAIM_VALUES` is) is refused (403) naming the bound, never cut to it. Reading them in the engine, under the lock the body runs under, is what keeps a `drop` and `create` landing between the grant and the lock from running a body with claims nobody bound.

*`--call-max-rows`* (`Database::set_call_max_rows`, 100 000 unless told). The rows left are an atomic of the database, set for the call and given back by a guard (`Bounded`) as the body returns or unwinds: given back only on a return, a hook's panic left the bound set, and every later statement of the database, a plain `get` among them, was refused as a call past its rows until it was opened again. It is charged in `matching_ids_to` -- with no filter the rows taken, an index's exact answer its rows, the candidates and the scan each row tested, the loop stopping at the bound -- by the ordered walks (`walk_order`, `walk_composite`), their rows walked, and wherever else a statement reads rows: `near`'s answer and the rows its filter tested or an index named (`filtered_near`), `match`'s answer or `rerank`'s candidates, both sides of `fuse`, the rows a facet over `match` counts (`matched_set`), each `lookup` level's children -- a bucket, or the run of an index over several fields walked (`kept_children`) -- and the children `required` tests (`retain_with_children_to`). The first cut charged the scan and the indexes alone, and a call read past its bound through `near`, `match` or a `lookup` within their own 10 000, or with none. An insert's unique probe is not counted. Past it the call is a query error naming the bound and its block is put back. Outside a call the bound is `usize::MAX`: a comparison a row in the two loops against a value read once a statement; in a build without the feature, a constant.

*The definition's checks* (`Database::body_checked`, beside `depends`). Each field a `set` writes, and each a `set`, a `del` or a `get` over one collection filters on, is held by its collection -- a select list, an order, a `having` and a join's or `lookup`'s names may be aliases, which only running the statement tells, and are its own checks' at the call. Where the body writes, every `get`, `set` and `del` of it reads its rows through an index -- an equality, an `in` or a range over `id` or a field an ordered index keeps (a hash index's, equalities and `in` alone), an index over several fields led by one, a pick an ordered index walks, `near`, `match` or `fuse`, or a `get` of the first rows with no filter and no order -- or the definition says `scans`. The walk asks the parser's conjunct helpers with a `null` for every parameter and claim, which they take for any value.

*Listing.* `statements` answers the server's token a row a statement -- `name`, `version` (`[history, change]`), `signature`, `writes`, `comment`, `broken` -- and `describe statement` adds `answers`, `text` and `depends`. A scoped token is told what it may call by `/_whoami`'s `calls`, `statements` refused (403) and `describe statement` the 404 a missing one gets.

*Authority* (fennec-http's `access`). A policy line `<statement>[, <statement> ...] call [for <role>]` grants calling (`call_line`): `call` beside another grant or a `where` is refused, `*` is refused with or without a role, and a name no statement may have is refused, each at startup. `Scope::calls` holds what the token's roles are granted. `Scope::rewrite` passes a call it grants -- one it does not is the 404 a missing statement gets, in the same words -- refuses `as` (403) and binds the token's claims in; a definition and a drop stay schema changes, refused. Under the write lock, `access::judge_call` asks `callee` again and holds the body to the policy's `append-only` collections (an update or delete in one, 403), a broken statement's why told as "it runs again once it is defined again"; `access::run_as` runs the call with no scope installed -- the one statement run outside `within()` -- its answer unmasked and its refusals told (`told_call`): a guard's `reason` kept, a duplicate "met a unique value taken", the body's other errors "could not run", `--call-max-rows`'s as it is, while the server's token is told the engine's words. `/query`, keyed or not, and `/batch` go through both, and so does `run_bound`, for a token whose rules read another collection. The order a scoped call is answered in: the tenant and the token (`route_tenant`), `--call-rate` (429), the grant (404), `as` (403), then under the lock the statement (404), the claims (403), the arguments and `check` (400), `append-only` (403) and a broken statement (409). Two places differ from the design's sentence: `as`, the token's own mistake, is refused where the token meets the statement, before its existence is asked, and `append-only`, which needs the definition the lock holds, after `check`, as the table of statuses orders it.

*A scoped `/batch`* holds one call and nothing beside it: two calls, or a call beside any statement, is 400 before a grant is asked; the server's token runs any `/batch`, its calls in its block.

*`--call-rate`* (`calls::admitted`): a token bucket for each caller in each database -- its subject with its issuer, and the `Database` the call runs in, a tenant's own on a node -- refilled at the rate and holding a second's worth, charged once a request holding a call before the grant is asked, answered 429 with `Retry-After` and `retry_after` in the body, the server's token not held. Keyed by the caller alone, tenant A's `sub` "1" spent tenant B's. The callers are held in two generations (`Callers`) that turn each second, the time a bucket takes to refill: a turn forgets those who did not call for a whole window, whose buckets are full again, so forgetting hands back nothing; past 16 384 callers within the last two windows a new one waits for the turn (429), and none still counting is forgotten. The first cut emptied the table past 4 096 callers, as MCP's is, which gave each its second back: a caller holding enough tokens of other subjects reset its own limit by flooding. 100 a second unless told -- the design left the figure open: more than a person's app makes, and a service that calls more says `--call-rate 0`.

**Slice 6, the SDKs, the golden files and types.** Every SDK has `call`, and the builder renders it, held to `integrations/builder-golden.json`. No Rust runs a call differently: a call is the `call <name> $1` slice 3 judged, sent over `/query` as any text is.

*The text.* `call(name, args, { claims })` makes a call, and its text method `toCall` the pair `[text, params]`, as `toInsert` does: `call <name>` for no arguments (absent or null), `call <name> $1` for an object (an empty one is still one argument), and ` as $n` after them for claims, numbered after the arguments (`call mine $1 as $2`, `call stamp as $1`). The arguments and the claims are parameters, normalised as a document is; the one thing written into the text is the name, checked by the engine's own rule (`named::valid_name`: ASCII letters, digits and `_`, a letter first, 59 bytes) before anything is sent, so it is the call's injection boundary, as `ident` is a query's. The refusals are three messages every SDK repeats to the word: ``invalid statement name: "<name>"`` (quoted as `invalid collection name` quotes), `a call's arguments are an object of values by name` and `a call's claims are an object of values by name`. **What CLAUDE.md would gain**, a section *Named statements* after *Writes*, each rule linking here:

> **[A named statement is a record of its database, checked against its schema and marked broken rather than failing an open.](docs/design/named-statements.md#definitions)** `create statement` writes record kind 13, a write `writes_in` counts, undone in a block (`Undo::Statement`), kept by every image with its grammar epoch and its tree's digest, and applied by a replica; its version is `(history, change)`. A body holds `get`, `insert`, `set ... where` and `del ... where` alone -- no `put`, no documents from a parameter, no `else set`, no schema change, no `call`, no `_` collection -- its last statement names its fields, and a writing scan says `scans`. A schema change breaking one is refused at its commit; an internal writer, a changed digest or a build without the feature marks it broken. A replacement keeps whether it writes, its claims and its answer's fields.
>
> **[A call runs its statement's text with the statement's own authority.](docs/design/named-statements.md#authority)** It is judged tenant, token, grant, existence, claims, arguments, `check`. `scoped()` passes a `call` only where a policy line names it (404 otherwise, as a statement no database holds; `* call` is refused), binds its declared claims from the token (a claim missing, null, empty or of another type is 403) and refuses `as` and an `append-only` update or delete; it is the one statement run outside `within()`, alone in a scoped token's `/batch`, and a scoped caller is told a refusal's kind, `at` and `reason` alone.
>
> **[A call is one block, a write at every site, its arguments values.](docs/design/named-statements.md#calls)** `Statement::Call` is never read-only before the lock; only the read-only executor, over `&Database`, reads a definition again and answers a read. Arguments are checked against the signature (400) and bound by place, never spliced; the answer is the last statement's; a call examines at most `--call-max-rows` rows and a subject makes at most `--call-rate` a second. A sync replica refuses `call` and `create statement`.
>
> **[Every surface names a call alike.](docs/design/named-statements.md#surfaces)** `POST /_call/<name>`, FennecQL's `call`, SQL's `CALL`, the SDKs' `call` (Dart's `callStatement`) held to the golden file; MCP's `call_<name>` tools, a writing one under `--mcp-write` and an answer past the bounds put back; counted as the shape `call <name> $1`, traced with `db.stored_procedure.name`, and no metric label names one. Readers upgrade before a primary defines one.

And *Declared schemas*' "plans additions alone" becomes "plans additions, and a statement's replacement where its contract holds".

## What it would measure

No figure above is this feature's own. What each slice owes, in turns against main: a call against the `/batch` of its statements under the server's token (the parse skipped: a row by id parses in 0.8-0.9 us and a filtered `get` in 2.4-2.5, [http.md](http.md#parse-cache), [fennecql.md](fennecql.md#sql-joins-subqueries-and-schema)) and under a scoped token (the scope's rewrite and `Check` skipped: a scoped `set` costs 1.6-1.9 us more than the server token's in process, [access.md](access.md#scoped-tokens)) -- `make policy-bench` and `make requests-bench`; eight clients' transfers a second as a call and as a scoped `/batch` (`make counters-bench`'s shape); a held claim as a call against `make queue-bench`'s; an open of 1 000 statements, each parsed and its digest checked (`make opens-bench`); a schema change's commit over a collection 1 000 statements depend on; the row count's cost on a call's scan; the module's bytes with and without `statements` (`make wasm-sizes`), and the server's, the router's and the shell's. Every path that holds no call -- a `get`, a `put`, a `/batch` -- must measure as it did: a statement form costs the parser a word and the engine a match arm, the policy a line kind it skips, and a schema change a look at a table that is empty.

## Decisions for the owner

1. **Where a definition lives.** *Recommended: in the database file*, a record of kind 13, replicated, archived, moved and restored with the data and checked against its schema; a tenant's statements are its own. The alternative, the server's policy file, is one API for every tenant of a node and goes unchecked when a schema changes.
2. **Whose authority a call runs under.** *Recommended: the statement's text, with typed declared claims* -- granted by name, no default grant, claims missing when null, empty or mistyped, the one statement outside `within()`. The alternatives -- the caller's rules (invoker), their intersection with the statement's, a role per statement -- each leave a transfer unable to do what it is for or put a second layer between the reader and what a statement may do.
3. **A replacement that changes the contract** -- whether it writes, the claims it reads, the fields it answers. *Recommended: refused, written as `drop` and `create`*, so no grant starts meaning more without a line of the policy changing, and the `drop` leaves every grant naming it logged. The alternative: allowed, logged as an audit event of its own, which keeps a statement's name and grants through such a change and asks operators to read the log.
4. **Sync replicas in the first version.** *Recommended: `call` and `create statement` refused in both runners*, from the slice that brings the syntax; the app calls its server over HTTP and the streams bring back what the call wrote. The alternative, forwarding from the replica, needs settling by the collections a body writes, a barrier in the queue and an asynchronous native path ([above](#forwarding-calls-from-a-sync-replica)), each a scenario of its own.
5. **The browser module.** *Recommended: the record in every build; defining and calling in a `statements` feature outside `full`, refused in the shipped module unless measured within a budget the owner sets* -- 3 KB brotli is suggested, against the review's 6-9 KB estimate, which would keep them out of the module for now. The alternatives: core in every build, which pays 6-9 KB for every page and gives a Durable Object calls; or never in the module.

## Write-bound statements (F2)

**Status: slice 1 built ([below](#what-f2-has-built)): the form reads into its tree, and every way of running a statement refuses it; slices 2 to 6 designed, not built.** A named statement bound to the writes of one collection, run in the block of the write that woke it, once a row, with that row as `new.` and `old.`: a ledger line, a counter, a denormalized field, kept with the write that made them in the record that holds it. This section decides what [F2's paragraph](#what-it-does-not-do) left open. It changes no rule of CLAUDE.md now; the rule comes with the slices that build it ([below](#what-claudemd-would-gain-from-f2)).

```fennecql
create statement paid_ledger
  after set orders where new.status = 'paid' and old.status = 'open'
  comment 'A ledger line and the debit for each order as it is paid'
as begin
  get accounts select id where id = new.account limit 1 require 1 else 'no such account';
  insert ledger {order: new.id, account: new.account, amount: new.total, at: now()};
  set accounts {balance: balance - new.total} where id = new.account
end
```

A customer's token granted `update(status)` on its own orders and `paid_ledger wake`, and nothing on `ledger` or `accounts`, pays an order with a `set`; the ledger line and the debit land in that write's block, or nothing does.

### The form of a binding

**A clause of `create statement`, not an object of its own.** `create [or replace] statement <name> after <event> [or <event> ...] <collection> [where <condition>] [comment '<text>'] [scans] as <body>`, an event being `insert`, `set` or `del` -- SQL's `update` and `delete` read as `set` and `del` there, as they do as statements ([fennecql.md](fennecql.md#sql-spellings)). The binding is its statement's: its name is the statement's, its record the statement's record, its digest covers it, and `drop statement` takes both away. Rejected:

- *`create binding <b> on orders after set call ledger(new.id, new.total)`*, a binding naming a callable statement: two records with a dependency between them, the condition that wakes a body and the body in two texts read apart -- where F1's one rule is that the text is the authority -- and the statement it names callable, so a token granted `ledger call` would write the ledger line with no order paid. Refusing a bound statement's calls would leave the second object nothing to add.
- *SQL's `CREATE TRIGGER`*, refused naming `create statement ... after` without consuming its word, as `CREATE FUNCTION` is: PostgreSQL's trigger runs a function as whoever fired it unless that function is `SECURITY DEFINER`, before, after or instead of the write, for each row or each statement, and fires the triggers its own writes wake. Read as ours, each of those would mean something else, which the SQL-spellings rule forbids.
- *`before`*, a binding changing the document as it is written (PostgreSQL's `BEFORE ROW` trigger returning a new row). A document is encoded before the write lock where no hook rewrites it ([engine.md](engine.md#prepared-blocks), `Hook::rewrites`) and judged as written by a scoped token's rules (`before_overwrite`); a field worked out from its own row is a generated field, a proposal of its own.

**Events are what happened to a row, never the statement that did it.** A counter of orders asks whether an order was made, not whether its client said `put` or `insert`, and the write path knows the effect already (`WriteOp`):

| The write | `insert`: a row made | `set`: a row written over | `del`: a row taken out |
|---|---|---|---|
| `insert`, `insert ... if absent` | each row it makes, over an expired row too | -- | -- |
| `insert ... if absent else set` | each row it makes, over an expired row too | each live row it sets | -- |
| `put` | a document naming no id, an id not held, or an id whose row is past its `@ttl` time | a document naming an id whose row is alive | -- |
| `set`, picked or not | -- | each row it sets | -- |
| `del`, picked or not | -- | -- | each row it deletes |
| REST's routes, MCP's `write`, a held write, a call's body | as the statement each is | | |
| `@ttl`'s sweep; an expired row a write clears for its `@unique` value | -- | -- | none: every read left the row out at its time |

`new.` is the row as the write leaves it -- a `put`'s document, a `set`'s row as set -- and `old.` the row as it stood, the document `before_overwrite` is handed. An `insert` binding reads `new.` alone, a `del` binding `old.` alone, a `set` binding both. `new.` is the spelling an upsert's `else set` and a policy's transitions already give the row as written ([writes.md](writes.md#upserts), [access.md](access.md#scoped-tokens)); `old.` is new, since a body's bare names are the rows of the collections it writes. A statement may bind several events of one collection (`after insert or set orders`) and reads only what every one of them has: `old.` in a binding of `insert or set` is refused at definition, as `new.` in one of `set or del`, never read as `null` for the event lacking it, whose comparison would be unknown and wake nothing without a word. One collection a statement: `new.` names one schema's fields, and two collections are two statements.

**An expiry is no event, and a binding promising to see one is refused.** A row past its `@ttl` time is gone to every read at that time ([indexes.md](indexes.md#ttl)) and taken out later by the sweep, which wakes nothing (below). The write path marks a write over such a row's id an update -- `put` reads the expired row as `old` and writes over it (`gone`, `WriteOp::Update`) -- but a binding is told what every read saw: a row made, an `insert` with no `old.`. Taken from the raw `WriteOp`, a `set` binding would read an `old.` no read could, and an `insert` counter would miss the row. So no binding ever sees a row expire: not by the sweep, not by a write clearing an expired `@unique` holder, not as a write over one. What that leaves unkept is a `del` binding's promise to see every row its collection loses -- a count kept by `after insert` and `after del` would count every expired row for ever -- so `after del` on a `@ttl` collection is refused at definition, and `create index ... @ttl` on a collection an `after del` binding is bound to is refused at its commit, naming the binding, as any schema change breaking a statement is; an internal writer making the collection `@ttl` marks the binding broken, which refuses its writes (below). `insert` and `set` bindings hold on a `@ttl` collection: every row made is an `insert`, and a `set` never finds an expired row, every filter having `alive` ANDed in. An `after expire` event is [the owner's](#f2s-decisions-for-the-owner) and left out.

**The condition is a filter over the pair.** `where` reads `new.<field>`, `old.<field>` and `now()` alone -- a bare name has no row to be read from, and is refused -- and is bound to the collection as a read's filter is ([indexes.md](indexes.md#bound-filters)): each field compares in its collation and its case fold as a `get` compares it (`Field::text_order`), so `new.status = 'PAID'` over a `nocase` field wakes where `get orders where status = 'PAID'` finds the row. No inner `get`, aggregate or `expired()` in it: a read of another collection is the body's guard (`get ... require`), whose refusal can say why.

**The body is F1's, with three rules more.** It holds what a callable statement's holds -- `get`, `insert`, `set ... where` and `del ... where`, never `put`, a parameter's documents, `else set`, a schema change, `call` or a `_` collection ([definitions](#definitions)), reading through an index or saying `scans` -- its `new.<field>`s and `old.<field>`s numbered as places in the order they first appear, so the body is the same `Expr::Param`s every statement holds and the engine's paths take it unchanged. A bound statement has no parameters, no `claims`, no `check`, no `public` and no parentheses: its values are the row's, its condition the `where`, and its writes are on behalf of whoever wrote the row, the server's own token included, which carries no claim. Then:

- *Its rows go nowhere*, so `returning` is refused, and so is a `get` without `require`: a guard is the one thing a read does there.
- *It writes no collection it is bound to.* A write into the rows its writer just wrote would change what the writer was answered (`returning`) and what its `require` counted, and a `set` over the collection its writer is setting would move rows the writer is still walking -- the Halloween problem. A field worked out from its own row is a generated field.
- *It writes no collection another statement is bound to.* A binding's writes wake nothing ([depth one](#a-bindings-authority)), so a binding inserting into `ledger` while another is bound to `ledger`'s inserts would leave the second asleep for every line the first wrote. Refused at definition in both orders -- a binding writing a bound collection, and a binding of a collection one writes -- naming the other, as a policy's rules reading each other's collections are refused at startup (`ring`, [access.md](access.md#rules-reading-another-collection)). PostgreSQL's triggers cascade, and SQLite's too (its `recursive_triggers`, off by default, stops only a trigger waking itself), which makes what a write does a graph to walk; a refusal can be lifted later, a cascade never taken back.

A collection is bound by at most 16 statements, refused past it, never cut ([crates.md](crates.md#limits)), and a collection the database calls its own (`declared::own`, a `_` name) by none.

### When a binding runs

**At the end of the statement that woke it, inside its block, before the block lands.** `run_one` runs a statement -- each row through the hooks (`before_write`, `before_overwrite`, `after_write`, `answering`) and the `@unique` check, its `require` counted and its answer made, as today -- then the bindings its rows woke, then spills where the block is due, and the block lands as one record: a crash, a replica and a subscriber see the write and what it woke together or neither ([engine.md](engine.md#blocks)). After the statement and never inside its loop over rows: `require` counts the statement's own rows, `returning` answers them as the statement wrote them, and no binding moves a row the statement has yet to walk. A `/batch`'s next statement reads what the bindings wrote, as it reads what the statement before it wrote; the writer's `Fennec-Seq` is the block's, the derived writes among it; an `Idempotency-Key`'s row lands in the same block, so a retry is answered what the first try was and wakes nothing again. A binding never changes the document its writer wrote, so a block prepared beside the readers lands the bytes it encoded, and the bindings are read under the write lock, from the table as it stands there, as a call's definition is. Nothing a binding does is past a block's undo -- its writes are rows the block's marks take back, and op 3 (below) is a definition `Undo::Statements` puts back as op 1's -- so `Statement::fits_block` is as it was.

**Once a row, in the order the statement wrote them, and on each row its bindings in name order.** Each row is asked each binding's condition as it is written, beside `after_write` -- the condition reads `new.`, `old.` and `now()` alone, nothing the statement is still changing -- and only a row passing one is kept, with its pair and the bindings it passed (`Database::woken`, asked only where the collection's mask holds the event, [below](#what-a-binding-costs-a-write)): a `set` over a million rows of which twelve are paid keeps twelve pairs, never a million `old` rows cloned for a check at the end. At the statement's end each kept row runs the bindings it passed, each body as a call's body runs, each statement through `run_one` with the row's values by place: rows first, then names, as PostgreSQL fires the `AFTER ROW` triggers a statement queued row by row and same-event ones by name ([trigger behavior](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/trigger-definition.html)). A run sees what the rows before it, and the bindings before it on its row, wrote. Once a statement -- every row handed to one run, PostgreSQL's transition tables (`REFERENCING NEW TABLE`) -- was weighed: a `put` of 1 000 likes would bump a post's counter once rather than write it 1 000 times into one record. FennecQL has no relation to hand a body, and an `in` over the `new.id`s is a list where a row's fields are the point; it is a follow-up of its own form, once a bulk write's record shows the cost. SQLite runs row triggers alone ([CREATE TRIGGER](https://www.sqlite.org/lang_createtrigger.html)).

**Bounded, refused past it, never cut.** A statement wakes at most `named::MAX_WOKEN` rows (10 000, the number `near`, `match` and a facet hold to), a row counted once whatever number of bindings it passed: the 10 001st row passing a condition refuses the write as it is written, naming the bound, and its block is put back -- never landed with the first rows' derived writes, and never more than 10 000 pairs held. The runs of one statement examine at most `--call-max-rows` rows together, its rows the thread's (`call::LEFT`): a fresh budget for a statement a client sent, set as the first run starts and given back as the last ends, on a panic too; inside a call's body, what the call has left, never a fresh budget a body could multiply by its statements. So the write lock a write holds is bounded by its rows and that budget. `now()` is the waking statement's, read once as its first row is asked a condition, so the condition and every run of one write read one time. A mass `del` of a bound collection is split by its writer, or the binding dropped first; a load goes in unbound (below).

**What wakes one, and what never does.** `run_one` is the one place a binding wakes, and every statement a client, an app or a call sends passes there: `/query`, `/batch`, REST's routes, MCP's `write`, a held write's look, a call's body, the native library, the shell. The writers holding no client's write never reach it, or run unbound:

- *Never reaching it.* A replica's `apply` and an archive's restore take records, not statements, and the record holds what the binding wrote: running it again would write it twice. An open, an image adopted, a move's install and a promotion load a file. A compact and the graph keeper write no document. `@ttl`'s sweep (`Database::sweep`, which deletes through `delete`, beneath every statement) and a write clearing an expired row for its `@unique` value (`erase`) take out rows every read left out at their time: a binding over expiry would fire when the sweeper got there, not when the row expired, and [F3](#what-it-does-not-do)'s scheduled statements are the form for acting on time.
- *Unbound* (`Database::unbound(|db| ...)`, as `internally` runs a writer that cannot refuse a schema change, and implied by it). `--follow`'s mirror, whose collections copy another database's rows -- their derived rows are their source's, and a copy again would wake every row once more. `fennec import`, a load, whose derived rows are a backfill the operator runs as a statement. A declared schema's migrations (`declared::migration_texts`, a `rebuild`'s `set` among them), which write what their text says and not what a binding declared beside them would make of it in the plan's order. The sync's apply of a stream's rows, and its remake.

The CVEs' danger -- one authority's code run by another's action -- is closed here at definition, which the server's token alone makes, so no internal writer runs anyone else's text. What the two lists keep an internal writer from is writing derived rows twice, or at a time no write chose.

### A binding's authority

**Its definer's -- the text -- never the writer's.** A bound statement's body runs as `run_as` runs a call, its writes checked by no token's scope, so a customer's `set` writes a ledger line no write rule of the customer's grants -- once a `wake` line grants the binding (below). Its authority is its text, defined by the server's token alone as every definition is. None calls one: `call` of a bound statement is the 404 a missing one is to a scoped token and a 400 naming why to the server's, a policy's `call` line naming one is logged as the server starts and grants nothing, and MCP lists no tool for it.

**A scoped token wakes only the bindings its policy grants it, by name.** Without a grant, any token that may write `orders` would write whatever `orders`' bindings write with no line of the policy naming them, and a statement bound later would widen what a grant made for something else lets a token cause: what F1's grants by name, and its refusal of a replacement widening a statement ([decision 3](#decisions-for-the-owner)), keep out. So a policy line `<statement>[, <statement> ...] wake [for <role>]` grants waking, as a `call` line grants calling:

```text
# statements     access   role
paid_ledger      wake     for customer
count_likes      wake     for member
```

`wake` stands alone on its line, `* wake` is refused with or without a role, and a line naming a statement no database holds, or a callable one, is logged and grants nothing, as `call`'s lines are (`calls::log_unheld`). A scoped token's write that wakes a binding its roles are not granted is refused (403) before the binding runs, its block put back: a binding never skips, and woken it runs or its write is refused. It is asked as a binding wakes -- a row of the statement passing its condition -- not of every write of its event: a courier moving an order's `location` wakes no `paid_ledger` and needs no grant, and one able to set `status` to `paid` is refused unless granted. The 403 names no binding to the token, as an ungranted statement is no name to it (F1's 404); the audit's refusal line names it (`audit::refusal`), for the operator writing the grant. `/_whoami` lists a token's `wakes` beside its `calls`. A scoped token's call is held the same, before its body runs: `judge_call` refuses (403) one whose body may wake, by the collections and events it writes, a binding the caller is not granted -- a call's writes are fixed by its text, so what they may wake is known before they run, where a write's rows are not. The server's token wakes every binding, as it calls every statement; the native library and the shell hold no policy.


**The writer's own write is judged first, by its own rules.** The writer's statement runs within its scope as ever -- `scoped()`, the field lists (`fields::hold`, `fields::write`), the `Check` hook over each row with its transitions, `append-only` -- so a row its rules refuse is a 403 before any binding is asked, and a write that wrote no row wakes nothing. Then the engine runs the bindings its rows woke:

- *`Hook::waking`*, asked of each binding a statement woke once the statement's rows are written and before the binding's first run, handed its name, the rows that woke it and the collections its body updates or deletes in, with the writer's scope in place: where the policy meets a binding. The `Check` hook refuses (403) a binding the token is not granted (`wake`, above) and one updating or deleting in an `append-only` collection, since `append-only` binds every scoped token ([access.md](access.md#scoped-tokens)), and charges its runs to the subject's `--call-rate` (below). `judge_call` asks the same of each binding a call's body may wake.
- *`Hook::writer_only`*, asked once a hook as `rewrites` is: a hook judging whoever wrote rather than what was written -- the `Check` hook -- says so, and the engine passes over it for a binding's writes. The other hooks run on them: a plugin generating embeddings writes them into a binding's insert as into anyone's, and fennec-server's guard of a mirrored collection refuses a binding writing into one, putting the writer's block back. A flag the engine reads, rather than a scope taken away and given back around each run, which a panic in a body would leave taken away -- as F1's slice 3 found its row bound left set.

**A scoped writer's runs are charged to its `--call-rate`.** A binding's run is a body run under the write lock on a token's behalf, as a call's is, so each run a scoped token's write wakes is charged to its subject's bucket in that database (`calls::admitted`), the server's token unheld: past it the write is answered 429 with `Retry-After` and `retry_after`, its block put back. A write waking more runs than a full bucket holds -- a second's worth -- would wait for ever, so it is refused (400, naming `--call-rate` and the runs), never told to come back, as a quota where waiting makes no room is a refusal ([quotas.md](quotas.md#quotas-in-the-sync)). A call's runs are its caller's, charged beside the call.

So access's "a scoped write run outside `within()` goes unchecked, a named statement's call alone" gains a second case, a binding's writes, held by the same kind of test: each part taken out, the transfer's sum broken or a write refused.

**`new.` is the writer's document.** Whatever a binding does with `new.<field>`, a token that may write that field chooses: a binding debiting `new.account` debits the account a customer's order names, so the policy over the bound collection is the binding's guard -- `orders` insertable only `where account in $jwt.accounts` -- with the binding's own condition and guards, as a callable statement's `where`s are a call's. The writer's identity reaches a binding the same way: a policy holding `new.by = $jwt.sub` over the row the writer writes (a transition) makes `new.by` the writer, which the binding copies into its audit line. A binding reading the writer's claims (`$jwt.sub`) was weighed: the server's token, an app's native library and an internal writer carry none, so a binding reading one would refuse the server's own writes (F1's missing claim, 403) or read `null` into a filter, the hole a missing claim must never leave -- both worse than a row the policy holds already.

**What it reads goes nowhere.** A binding answers nothing: its last statement's rows are dropped and `returning` refused, so no writer is answered a row through it. What it writes is its definer's to show: a binding copying `old.email` into a collection a token reads shows it to that token, as a callable statement's answer is the statement's ([authority](#authority)). A guard's refusal tells its writer one bit and the `reason` the text wrote (below).

**Depth one.** A binding's writes wake nothing: its statements run through `run_one` with waking off, keeping no row; with a chain refused at definition, depth one never leaves a binding asleep without a word.

### When a binding refuses

**The block is put back whole, and the writer told a binding refused.** A binding's error -- a guard's `require` unmet (412), a `@unique` value taken (409), `MAX_WOKEN` or `--call-max-rows` (400), a broken binding (409), and for a scoped writer a binding not granted or reaching an `append-only` collection (403) and `--call-rate` (429, or 400 past a second's worth) -- is its writer's statement's: `run_one` returns it, and the block -- the statement, the bindings run before, a `/batch`'s statements before it -- is put back as any block whose statement fails ([engine.md](engine.md#blocks)). A tenant's quota is asked of the record as it lands, the derived rows' bytes and rows in it and charged to the writer's thread (`writing_here`), and its 507 names the bound alone, as ever ([quotas.md](quotas.md#tenant-quotas)). The status is the binding's error's, and `at` the writer's statement's place in its `/batch`, since the binding ran as part of it. The engine notes the binding that refused (`Database::woken`), and each writer is told:

| Writer | Told |
|---|---|
| the server's token, the native library, the shell | the engine's words after "bound statement `paid_ledger`, after `set` on `orders`, refused this write:", and `binding: "paid_ledger"` beside `at` and `reason` |
| a scoped token | the status, `at`, and the `reason` its guard's `else` wrote -- the definer's words, written to be told -- in "a write bound to this one refused it" (`told_bound`, as `told_call` tells a call's), never the binding's name or the engine's words |

A scoped writer is told no name because F1 tells a token no name it was not granted (a missing statement and an ungranted one are one 404): a name says what the schema does -- `fraud_check` -- and a unique clash's words name values the token may not read. The name and the words are the trace's and, past `--slow-ms`, the slow line's, under the request id. Each SDK's error gains `binding`, `null` but for the server's token.

**A binding that cannot run refuses the writes it binds.** A broken binding -- a text reading here as another statement, a dependency an internal writer took away, one a process with no reader installed holds unread -- is neither run nor passed over: a write of its event on its collection is refused (409, "the write would wake `paid_ledger`, which is broken: ... define it again or drop it"), its condition unasked, since a broken binding's condition is no more to be trusted than its body. F1's broken statement is refused to its callers; a binding's callers are its writers. Letting the write land without its rows leaves a ledger silently short. An ordinary writer cannot break one -- its schema change is refused at its commit, as for a callable statement -- and an internal writer that marks one broken writes unbound.

### A binding's record, replicas and upgrades

**Kind 13, op 3: a bound definition, which a binary from before refuses.** `[13][0][length][3][history][change][epoch][digest][name][depends][collection][events][text]`: op 1's fields, then the bound collection's name and its events (a byte: 1 `insert`, 2 `set`, 4 `del`) outside the text, so a build that cannot parse the text knows what it binds. Its drop is op 2, as any statement's. F1's reader refuses an op it does not read as corrupt ([slice 2](#what-is-built)), so an F1 binary -- a replica, an archiver, a node a tenant moves to, a standby a failover would promote -- stops at the first binding in words. Read as op 1, the record would be a callable statement whose text no longer reads, marked broken while the writes it binds land without their rows on the first F1 node a failover promoted: a misread the file format forbids ([storage.md](storage.md#file-format)). A new kind was weighed: kind 13 carries the version, the epoch, the digest, the table and `Undo::Statements` already, and an op is a number that binary refuses all the same. `writes_in` counts op 3 as one write as it counts op 1, so a replica's counter stays its primary's; an image writes a bound definition with op 3, a compact keeps it and `changes_in` passes over it. The canonical tree gains the binding's fields, each written only where it holds something, so no stored digest moves and `GRAMMAR_EPOCH` stays where it is (2), and `make fennecql-corpus` holds every text the repository holds to main's tree.

**A replica never runs a binding: the record holds what it wrote.** A follower applies the block -- the order paid, the ledger line, the debit -- as written, and op 3 into its table, so a promoted copy runs the bindings from its first write. `/_changes` tells the derived rows as the writes they are, in the change range of the write that made them; an archive holds them, and a restore and a move carry them. A query across tenants reads, and wakes nothing.

**Its dependencies are F1's, the bound collection among them.** `depends` names the bound collection and each field `new.` and `old.` read, beside what the body names: dropping `orders`, or its `status`, is refused naming the binding while it stands, and a `rebuild` lands where the binding still checks at its block's commit. A replacement keeps the kind -- a callable statement replaced by a bound one, or the other way, is refused naming the `drop` and `create` it needs, since a `call` or `wake` line naming it would name something else -- and a binding's contract, so a `wake` line grants what it granted: the collection and events it is bound to, and what it writes, each collection its body inserts into, sets or deletes in, by the kind of write (`Definition::breaks_contract`). Within it -- a condition, a value, a guard, a field a `set` writes -- a replacement needs no new grant, as a callable statement's within its contract needs none; one bound to another collection or event, or writing a collection or a kind of write it did not, is refused naming the `drop` and `create` it needs, whose `drop` leaves each `wake` line naming it logged, as F1's decision 3 has it. A condition widened is within it: the grant is to cause what the binding writes, and the grantee's own rules over the bound collection hold which rows it writes.

**Upgrades go one way, every reader before the first binding.** The order is F1's ([upgrades](#replicas-sync-the-router-and-upgrades)): archivers, routers, every node, and then a primary may define one. `make upgrade-check` gains a base, the last main before op 3, whose replica must stop at op 3 in words and connect again, whose archiver keeps the stream's bytes and takes no image of its own, whose `restore` and server refuse the archive and the file, whose router refuses a schema declaring a binding (400, nothing placed), and onto whose node a move fails at the install, the tenant left on its source. RELEASING.md's notes name `after` as the clause that moves the format.

### A binding beside sync replicas and the module

**A sync replica sends the write and receives what its bindings wrote through its streams: no new code refuses it.** A sync replica holds its shapes, never its server's statements -- the streams pass over kind 13 -- so it cannot know which of its writes wake a binding, and runs none. Its optimistic write is that write alone, queued and sent as any synced write, which the server runs in a block with the bindings it wakes. The derived rows come back through the shapes following their collections, in the record numbered as the write's own, so a shape past the write's `Fennec-Seq` holds them. A binding's refusal (412, 409) puts the optimistic write back as any refusal of it does, with its `reason`, and a 507 naming a `quota` is a quota's ([quotas.md](quotas.md#quotas-in-the-sync)). `NAMED` refuses a binding's definition on a synced database as it refuses any `create statement`. Rejected:

- *Refusing a synced write that would wake a binding*, by a code beside `NAMED`: the replica would need its server's bindings in the shapes' handshake to know them, and an app taking orders offline -- what a sync replica is for -- could not write a bound collection at all.
- *Running the binding optimistically on the replica*: its rows are a filtered part of its server's, so the debit would find no account its shapes do not hold and a guard would count the replica's rows -- why it refuses `GUARDED`, `PICKED` and `UPSERT_KEY` writes -- and its derived rows would differ from the server's until the streams replaced them.

`integrations/sync-scenarios.json` gains the cases before either runner changes ([native-and-sync.md](native-and-sync.md#sync-scenarios)): a synced `set` waking a binding, the derived row absent from the optimistic view and arriving through a shape following its collection; a binding's guard refusing, the write put back with its `reason`; a queued write sent again under its `Idempotency-Key` after its answer was lost, answered the first try's answer from `_idempotency` with its binding run once -- one ledger line in the stream, never two; a synced database refusing a binding's definition (`NAMED`). Both runners are expected to pass them as they are, the native core's apply of a stream's rows running unbound, where a local database could hold no binding anyway.

**The browser module runs none, and refuses the writes a binding binds.** Bindings are F1's `statements` feature, outside `full` and out of the module ([the browser module](#the-browser-module-and-embedded)), which refuses defining and calling already. But a page opens a server's file (`openFile`) and writes it, and a write there landing without its derived rows would go back to the server a ledger short. So the record's binding is core, as op 1's record is: every build reads op 3's collection and events into the collection's mask, and a build that cannot run bindings refuses a write of a bound event on a bound collection before it changes anything, the condition unread ("`orders` has statements bound to its writes, which this module does not run: write it through a server"), as `collate::refuse` refuses a statement reaching a chunk the page lacks. A Durable Object running the module writes no bound collection, and a FennecSync replica holds no binding (above). Measured in its slice (`make size-report BASE=origin/main`), against the 824 bytes brotli op 1's record cost.

### Held writes wake on derived rows

**A derived write wakes the groups reading what it wrote, through what is there already.** `Waits::wrote` is called by the database's watcher with the collections the landed block noted (`changed_collections_since`), and a binding's writes are notes of that block: a claim held on `ledger` looks when a `set orders` lands a ledger line, the row going to the group ranking it first (`waits::settle`), with nothing new in fennec-http. A held `set` or `del` on a bound collection is held as ever and wakes its bindings when it writes, in its look's block; whether it wrote is asked of the database (`waits::Mark`), which counts the derived writes too and is right to, a write writing no row of its own waking nothing. `--max-memory`'s ceiling and `grows` ask what a write may grow by its bindings as by its own statement: a `del` of a collection whose binding inserts is held as the insert it makes (`over_ceiling`, as it asks `call_body` of a call). A test holds the wake: a claim held on `ledger` answered by a `set orders`, failing with the bindings' notes taken out.

### Watching bindings

- *The slow line* names the bindings a request woke, each with its version and rows (`bound: [["paid_ledger", [h, c], 12]]`); its time is the request's, which holds theirs. A scoped writer's refused write is logged with the binding's name and words, which its writer was not told.
- *Traces*: a child span a binding a statement -- never a row -- its `db.operation.name` `bound`, `db.stored_procedure.name` the name, `fennec.rows_woken` its rows. The engine times a binding's runs together, two clock reads a binding a statement and none where nothing woke, and hands them over in `Database::woken`, since the trace's thread-local is fennec-http's.
- *`/_stats/statements`* counts each binding as a shape of its own, `after paid_ledger`, its count the statements that woke it, its rows theirs, its time its runs', beside the writer's shape, whose time holds it: an operator tuning a slow `set orders` sees what of it was the ledger. Read as every shape is ([http.md](http.md#statement-counts)).
- *`/_metrics`*: `fennec_bound_runs_total` and `fennec_bound_refused_total`, no label naming a binding or a collection ([http.md](http.md#metrics)).
- *The audit*: `create statement ... after`, its replacement and its drop are schema changes logged by their shapes (`audit::statement`), the line naming the bound collection and events. A run is no event, as a write is none; a scoped writer refused through `Hook::waking` is a 403 logged as any.

### What a binding costs a write

**A collection with no binding pays one byte's look a writing statement, never a row's.** Each collection keeps a byte, `Collection::bound`, the events its bindings wake, set wherever the table or the collections change (`Database::rebind`: a definition, a drop, `Undo::Statements`, an open, a replica's apply, an image adopted), and the write path reads it once a statement, where it asks `unique` once a statement today. Where the byte lacks the event, no pair is kept, no `old` cloned and no condition asked, `woken` stays empty and `run_one` looks at its length. Where it holds it, each row is asked the conditions as it is written, and only a row passing one keeps its pair -- a `set` the `old` it reads for the hooks already, a `del` the document `after_write` is handed -- so what a write holds is bounded by `MAX_WOKEN`, never by the rows it walks. Held by `a_write_to_an_unbound_collection_keeps_no_row` -- a count of the pairs kept, under `#[cfg(test)]`, staying 0 over every write form on one collection while another is bound -- and measured in turns against main, as F1's slices 3 and 5 were: in process and through `fennec_http::handle`, a `put`, an `insert`, a `set` and a `del` by id, a `put` of 1 000 documents and a `set` over a scan, each the median of nine rounds over five pairs, a pair run under someone else's load (`uptime`) discarded; `make requests-bench`; `make size-report` for the module and `fennec-server`. A path holding no binding measures as it did, or the slice is not done.

**What a binding costs is held to what it saves.** A `set` waking one binding that inserts a row, against the `/batch` of that `set` and that `insert` under the server's token -- the parse and a round trip spared, the condition and the pair paid -- and eight clients paying orders with the ledger bound, against the same as a scoped `/batch` (`make counters-bench`'s shape), which a scoped token could not send without a grant on `ledger`.

### What F2 has built

**Slice 1, the parser and the tree.** `create [or replace] statement <name> after <event> [or <event> ...] <collection> [where <condition>] [comment '<text>'] [scans] as <body>` reads into `fennec_core::named`'s tree: `Definition::after`, a `Binding` boxed so a callable definition carries a pointer for it -- the collection, the events as the record's bits (`Binding::INSERT`, `SET`, `DEL`: 1, 2, 4), the condition, and `places`, what each `Expr::Param(k)` of the body reads (`Place`: `new.` or `old.` and the field, a path into a `json` one its own place). An event is `insert`, `set` or `del`, SQL's `update` and `delete` reading as `set` and `del`, each once, in any order. The condition keeps `new.status` and `old.status` as the `Expr::Field`s they are written as, for slice 3 to bind to the collection as a read's filter is, its collation and case fold with it; the body's are numbered as places in the order they first appear (`Parser::defined_field`), so a body is the statements every path of the engine takes. Out of a binding, `new.` and `old.` read as on main: a path into a field of that name, and an upsert's document.

*The refusals the text alone answers*, each at the word it is about: `old.` where an event is `insert` and `new.` where one is `del`, in the condition and the body, never read as `null`; a bare name in the condition; a `$` of any kind (`$1`, `$a`, `$jwt.sub`), parentheses, `claims`, `check` and `public` (`BOUND_VALUES`); in the condition an inner `get` (at its `get`, before its own names would be refused as bare ones), an aggregate or `expired()` (`named::condition_refused`); in the body F1's refusals, and a `get` without `require` and any `returning` (`Definition::bound_refused`), and `new.` or `old.` where a name stands, a field a `set` writes (`Parser::pair_named`) -- an item of a list is an expression already, refused as one answering under no name; a collection whose name starts with `_`, at the parse rather than at the table, since the text alone answers it; an event named twice. SQL's words, unconsumed and naming the form: `create trigger` (`TRIGGER`), `drop trigger`, `before`, `when` for `where`, and `on` before the collection (`after update on orders`). A binding's own collection and a chain are slice 3's, as the plan has them: they ask what other statements are bound, which only the table holds.

### F2's risks

- *An internal writer forgotten*, running a binding twice or at a time no write chose. Most cannot: a replica, a load, a compact and the sweep never reach `run_one`. The rest are `Database::unbound`'s, each a case of the test above, and CLAUDE.md's rule names them, so a new one owes a line there.
- *A way in forgotten*, landing a write without its rows. `run_one` is the one door every statement passes; the test runs each way in.
- *`new.` trusted where its writer chooses it.* A binding is as safe as the policy over its collection: the reference and auth pages carry the debit with its policy line, and a binding's guards are where a definer checks what a row says.
- *Grants to keep.* A binding woken by a role with no `wake` line refuses that role's writes once a row passes its condition: loud, named in the audit, the price of a policy that says what a token may cause. A binding defined after the policy wakes for no scoped token until a line names it.
- *Write amplification.* A run a row: a `put` of 1 000 likes writes a post's counter 1 000 times into one record. Bounded by `MAX_WOKEN`, measured in slice 3; a binding over a whole statement is the follow-up if the record shows the cost.
- *The write lock held longer*, by a write's bindings: bounded by `MAX_WOKEN` and `--call-max-rows`, shown by the slow line and the spans.
- *Availability.* A broken binding stops its collection's writes until it is defined again or dropped, loud by choice; only an internal writer or a grammar change can break one, which the commit's check and the corpus rule keep rare.
- *An older node promoted* stops at op 3 rather than writing without the binding: the upgrade's order keeps the cluster writing.
- *The sync's optimistic view* shows a write before its derived rows, said on the sync page and held by a scenario.
- *CLAUDE.md's room*: 62 321 bytes of 64 000, and the rule below about 1 300, so slice 3 makes it room in F1's rules, as #462 made room before, every rule kept.

> **[A write-bound statement runs in the block of the write that woke it, once a row.](docs/design/named-statements.md#write-bound-statements-f2)** `create statement <n> after insert|set|del <c> [where ...] as ...` is kind 13's op 3, which a binary from before refuses, and a build or a server that cannot run one refuses the writes it binds. `run_one` alone wakes it, after the statement's rows, hooks and `require` and before the block lands, row by row, its bindings in name order; it is never called, its writes wake none and it writes no bound collection; its condition is asked as each row is written, a write over an expired row is an `insert`, and `after del` and `@ttl` refuse each other; past `MAX_WOKEN` woken rows or `--call-max-rows` a write is refused, never cut, and a refusal puts the block back, a scoped writer told its status, `at` and `reason` alone. A scoped token wakes only what a `wake` line grants it (403, never a skipped binding), its runs charged to `--call-rate`, and a replacement keeps the bound collection, events and what it writes. A replica's apply, a load, a compact and the sweep never reach it; `--follow`'s mirror, `fennec import`, a declared migration and the sync's apply run `Database::unbound`; a collection with no binding pays one byte's look a statement (`Collection::bound`).

And, with slice 4, *scoped tokens*' "a scoped write run outside `within()` goes unchecked, a named statement's call alone (`run_as`)" gains "and a binding's writes, which the `Check` hook passes over (`Hook::writer_only`) once `Hook::waking` has judged its `wake` grant"; the rule's `wake` and `--call-rate` sentence lands with that slice.

### F2's decisions for the owner

1. **The syntax.** *Recommended: a clause of `create statement`* -- one record, one text, a bound statement never callable. The alternative, `create binding` naming a callable statement, lets one statement be called and bound, at the price of a grant writing derived rows with no write behind them.
2. **A chain.** *Recommended: refused at definition, in both orders.* The alternative, depth one with chains allowed, leaves a binding asleep for every row another wrote, without a word.
3. **A broken binding.** *Recommended: its writes refused (409)* until it is defined again or dropped. The alternative lets them land without their rows, the gap in the log alone.
4. **What a scoped writer is told.** *Recommended: its status, `at` and `reason`, no name.* The alternative names the binding, which helps a client's developer and tells every token what the schema does.
5. **Expiry.** *Recommended: no binding sees a row expire* -- the sweep wakes nothing, a write over an expired row is an `insert`, and `after del` and `@ttl` refuse each other. The alternative, `after expire`, runs a binding when the sweeper gets there, at a time no write chose; F3's scheduled statements are the form for acting on time.
6. **Where a `wake` grant is asked.** *Recommended: as a binding wakes*, a row passing its condition, and for a call before its body by what it may wake. The alternative, every write of a bound event, needs a grant from every role writing the collection -- a courier moving an order's location granted the ledger -- for no binding it could wake.
7. **What `--call-rate` charges.** *Recommended: each run* a scoped writer's write wakes, refused past a full bucket. The alternative, one charge a binding a statement, lets one `put` of 10 000 rows run 10 000 bodies under the write lock for the price of one.
